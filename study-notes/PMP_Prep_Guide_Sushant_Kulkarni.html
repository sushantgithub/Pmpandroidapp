How PMI Thinks — The Core Mental Model

  • Proactive always beats reactive. If two answers look similar, pick the one that anticipates the problem rather than reacts to it.
  • A.R.T. sequence: Assess/Analyze → Review project documents → Take Action. Never jump straight to action.
  • Communicate first. Before any drastic action, communicate with stakeholders.
  • Collaborate, don't escalate. Escalate to the sponsor only when the issue is beyond the PM's authority, after you've tried to resolve it internally.
  • Process always applies. Even for "small" changes — Integrated Change Control, no exceptions.
  • Never fire/hire/terminate as a first response. Never as a second either.
  • Never bypass the team. PM does not delegate PM responsibilities to others.
  • Safety first — always. Safety issues stop all other work immediately.
  • The team estimates. The PM does not estimate for the team — SMEs provide estimates.
  • Face-to-face is best. For conflict, sensitive topics, and complex issues, always prefer face-to-face interaction.

Q Your project is behind schedule. A stakeholder demands you accelerate delivery without increasing budget. What is your FIRST step?
✅ Answer: C
PMI always favours communication and collaboration first. Assess the situation, communicate trade-offs and implications to the stakeholder, and collaboratively determine the best path forward. Crashing (adding resources) costs money — contradicting the "no budget increase" constraint. Scope reduction needs formal change control. Cutting corners violates quality standards and ethics.
Q A team member tells you the project budget might be exceeded next quarter if a vendor delivers late. What is this — and what do you do FIRST?
✅ Answer: B
"Might" signals uncertainty about a future event — this is a RISK, not an issue. First action: document it in the Risk Register. Then perform qualitative risk analysis to prioritise it. Then plan a risk response. The Issue Log is for problems that have already occurred. Escalation is premature without analysis.
Q A senior executive asks you to bypass change control for a minor scope addition she personally approved. What do you do?
✅ Answer: B
ALL changes must go through Integrated Change Control regardless of who requests it or how minor it seems. The PM's job is to follow the process — not circumvent it under authority pressure. Submitting a formal change request protects the project, the PM, and ensures the impact is properly assessed and approved.

Q During the exam, you encounter a network diagram question asking for the critical path. Activities: A(3)→B(4)→D(2) and A(3)→C(6)→D(2). What is the critical path duration?
✅ Answer: B
Path 1: A→B→D = 3+4+2 = 9 days. Path 2: A→C→D = 3+6+2 = 11 days. The critical path is the LONGEST path = A→C→D = 11 days. Activities on this path (A, C, D) have zero float. Activity B has 2 days of float (11−9).
Q You are 90 minutes into the exam and have answered 60 questions. You are on pace for how many questions in 240 minutes?
✅ Answer: B
60 questions in 90 min = 1.5 min per question. At that rate, 240 min ÷ 1.5 = 160 questions. You will NOT finish all 180 questions. You need to speed up to ~1.33 min/question. This is why time management practice is critical — many candidates know the content but fail on time.
Q On a Kanban board shown in a graphic question, the "In Review" column has 8 items but a WIP limit of 3. What does this indicate and what should the team do?
✅ Answer: B
A column exceeding its WIP limit signals a bottleneck. In Kanban, the response is to STOP pulling new work into the system and focus the team on clearing the bottleneck (swarming). Raising the WIP limit hides the problem. Removing the limit defeats the purpose of Kanban. Replacing people is never a first response.
Q A case study describes a hybrid project where the infrastructure component uses predictive methods and the software component uses Scrum. A question asks how the PM should handle a change request to the infrastructure scope. What is correct?
✅ Answer: B
In a hybrid project, the PREDICTIVE component follows predictive rules — formal change control. The infrastructure scope is predictive, so a formal change request through the CCB is correct. Only the software (Scrum) component would use backlog prioritisation. Hybrid means applying the right process to each component — not blending them randomly.

Why Was PMBOK 8 Created? — Evidence-Based

PMI surveyed ~64,000 practitioners globally. Key findings that drove PMBOK 8:

  • 88% wanted principle-based standard — but principles should be more actionable and directly linked to domains
  • 80% wanted the 5 Process Groups (Focus Areas) reintroduced — universally useful regardless of development approach
  • 79% wanted technical processes (ITTOs) integrated back into the guide — not removed
💡
Design rule: If a topic (e.g., stakeholder engagement) is already a performance domain, it should NOT also be a principle — avoid duplication. This is why PMBOK 8's 6 principles are broader and behavioural.

✅ Answer: C
Monitoring and Controlling is a Focus Area that runs IN PARALLEL with all other Focus Areas throughout the entire project — from initiation through closing. It is not sequential. Waiting until execution is complete or until problems arise would leave the project uncontrolled.

Key PMBOK 8 Terminology — Know These Definitions

TermPMBOK 8 Definition
ArtifactA template, document, output, or project deliverable. A product is an artifact that is produced, quantifiable, and can be an end item or component.
DeliverableAny unique and verifiable product, result, or capability to perform a service that is required to be produced to complete a process, phase, or project.
OutcomeAn end result or consequence of a process or project. Outcomes can include outputs and artifacts but have broader intent — focused on benefits and value.
ValueThe worth, importance, or usefulness of something. Different stakeholders perceive value differently — financial, social, environmental, reputational.
BenefitA gain realised by the organisation and beneficiaries through project outputs and resulting outcomes.
DisbenefitA negative consequence of project outputs — must be identified and managed alongside benefits (new in PMBOK 8 vocabulary).
Project Management TeamMembers of the project team directly involved in project management activities (broader than just the PM).
Development ApproachThe method used to create and evolve the product, service, or result — predictive, adaptive, or hybrid.
Life CycleThe series of phases a project passes through from start to completion. Different from the development approach.
TailoringThe deliberate adaptation of the PM approach, governance, and processes to make them more suitable for the given environment.

Q Which PMBOK 8 performance domain is the ONLY one with processes in all five Focus Areas (Initiating through Closing)?
✅ Answer: C
Governance is the only domain with processes in all 5 Focus Areas: Initiate Project/Phase (Initiating), Integrate & Align Plans (Planning), Manage Execution (Executing), Monitor & Control Performance (M&C), and Close Project/Phase (Closing). It is the integrating domain — like Integration Management in PMBOK 6. Stakeholders has Initiating but not Closing. Scope, Schedule, Finance, Resources, Risk have no Initiating or Closing processes.
Q A project delivers all scope on time and on budget, but the intended business benefits are never realised because market conditions changed. According to PMBOK 8, was this project successful?
✅ Answer: B
PMBOK 8 (and the Focus on Value principle) defines project success by whether it delivered VALUE and realised BENEFITS — not just whether outputs were delivered on time and on budget. A project that delivers all outputs but no value has technically failed by PMBOK 8 standards. This is a key mindset shift from PMBOK 6.
Q Which PMBOK 8 term describes a negative consequence of project outputs that must be identified and managed alongside benefits?
✅ Answer: B
A "Disbenefit" is a negative consequence of project outputs — a term formalised in PMBOK 8. For example, a new system may deliver efficiency benefits but create a disbenefit of staff reduction or training burden. Disbenefits must be identified in the Benefits Management Plan and managed just like benefits. Residual risk refers to remaining risk after responses; negative float relates to schedule.
Q According to PMBOK 8, what is the difference between an "outcome" and an "output"?
✅ Answer: B
An OUTPUT is what the project produces — the deliverable (e.g., a software application). An OUTCOME is the end result or change that the output enables (e.g., 30% reduction in processing time). PMBOK 8 emphasises that PMs must focus on outcomes and value, not just producing outputs. A perfect output that produces no desired outcome is a failed project.
Q A PM is tailoring the governance approach for a small internal project in an agile organisation. According to PMBOK 8, what level of governance is appropriate?
✅ Answer: B
PMBOK 8 explicitly states governance should be tailored: lightweight for adaptive/agile methods and small projects; moderate for hybrid; comprehensive for large predictive portfolios. A small internal agile project needs proportionate, lightweight governance — not the full apparatus of a large regulated project. But "no governance" is never correct — every project needs some governance framework.
📖 Worked Examples & Deep Dives

The 6 Principles — Applied to a Real Project Situation

Project: Implementing AI-powered credit scoring for a microfinance institution serving rural borrowers

Principle 1 — Adopt a Holistic View:
The team notices the AI model is trained on urban credit bureau data. Rural borrowers have different financial patterns (agricultural income cycles, informal credit). A holistic view reveals the model may systematically deny credit to exactly the customers the institution wants to serve.
→ Expand the model training to include rural financial behaviour data before launch.

Principle 2 — Focus on Value:
The project's KPI is "number of loan applications processed per day." The PM flags that processing 500 applications efficiently but approving 400 incorrect ones destroys value. Reframe the KPI to "default rate" and "customer satisfaction" — the actual value drivers.

Principle 3 — Embed Quality:
Rather than testing the AI model only at the end, add bias-testing checkpoints after every training iteration. Make "fairness metrics across demographic groups" part of the Definition of Done for each model version.

Principle 4 — Be an Accountable Leader:
When a team member discovers the model makes errors on agricultural borrowers, the PM does not blame the data scientist. She takes ownership: "We did not include this in our test cases. I own that. Here is how we fix it." This creates safety to surface problems early.

Principle 5 — Integrate Sustainability:
The institution serves 50,000 rural borrowers. A biased model that denies credit to creditworthy rural women would have profound social impact (disbenefits). The PM documents both the financial benefit AND the social risk in the Benefits Management Plan.

Principle 6 — Build an Empowered Culture:
The junior data scientist noticed the bias issue but was afraid to raise it. After the PM's Principle 4 response, he feels safe to raise two more concerns in the next sprint. The team's best ideas are now accessible.

Q Two team members have a technical conflict. You have spoken to each individually. What is your NEXT step?
✅ Answer: C
After meeting individually (correct first step), the next step is to bring both parties together for collaborative problem-solving — PMI's preferred conflict resolution technique. Escalation and forcing are last resorts. Documenting without resolution avoids the conflict entirely, which almost never resolves underlying issues.
Q During a sprint retrospective, the team identifies that they consistently overcommit to sprint goals. What should the Scrum Master do?
✅ Answer: B
The Scrum Master facilitates retrospectives and coaches the team — this is a coaching/process improvement situation. Overcommitment is an estimation and sustainable pace issue. Facilitating a team discussion on improving their estimation approach (story points, planning poker) and calibrating velocity accurately addresses the root cause. Telling the team to work faster violates sustainable pace principles.
Q A project manager discovers that a team member has been reporting task status as "complete" when work is only partially done. What should the PM do FIRST?
✅ Answer: B
A.R.T.: First, meet privately with the team member to understand why this is happening (Assess). There may be pressure to appear on track, misunderstanding of "done," or other root causes. Jumping to escalation, removal, or stakeholder announcements before understanding the situation is premature and could damage trust and morale unnecessarily.

Q A team member from a different cultural background rarely speaks up in meetings, and other members interpret this as disengagement. What should the PM do?
✅ Answer: B
This is a cultural intelligence scenario. In many cultures, speaking up in group meetings — especially to disagree — is inappropriate. The PM should recognise this, avoid misinterpreting silence as disengagement, and create alternative channels (one-on-one conversations, written input, smaller groups) where the team member can contribute comfortably. Telling them to change or reassigning them is culturally insensitive and violates the Respect value.
Q During a status meeting, the sponsor angrily demands to know why the project is behind schedule and interrupts the PM repeatedly. What demonstrates emotional intelligence?
✅ Answer: B
Self-regulation (staying calm) + empathy (acknowledging the concern) + social skills (presenting facts and proposing solutions) = emotional intelligence in action. Matching anger escalates conflict. Ending the meeting avoids the issue. Blindly agreeing abandons professional judgment. The EI response is calm, factual, and solution-oriented.
Q A vendor you are evaluating for a large contract sends you an expensive gift. Your organisation has no explicit gift policy. What should you do?
✅ Answer: C
Fairness and Honesty values of the PMI Code of Ethics apply. Even without an explicit policy, accepting an expensive gift from a vendor under evaluation creates an actual or perceived conflict of interest. The correct action: decline, document the offer, and consult with appropriate authority. "No policy" does not mean "no ethical obligation." The exam always rewards the option that avoids even the appearance of impropriety.
Q A PM discovers their team has been reporting task status as "on track" to hide delays, hoping to catch up. The sponsor is unaware. What should the PM do?
✅ Answer: B
Honesty is non-negotiable. The PM must report accurate status to the sponsor immediately — hiding delays violates the Honesty value and prevents the sponsor from making informed decisions. Separately, the PM must address the team culture that led to misreporting — understanding WHY they felt pressure to hide delays (psychological safety issue?). Public reprimand violates Respect and damages trust further.
Q Which of the following BEST demonstrates the PMI value of Responsibility?
✅ Answer: C
Responsibility means taking ownership of decisions and their consequences. Acknowledging your own error and proposing a solution demonstrates this. Misreporting (A) violates Honesty. Deferring all decisions (B) abdicates responsibility. Delegating difficult conversations (D) avoids accountability. The Responsibility value requires PMs to own outcomes — good and bad.

A.R.T. Framework — 5 Scenario Walk-throughs

Scenario 1: "The project is 3 weeks behind schedule."
Assess: Which activities are delayed? Is it the critical path? What caused the delay? (Resource shortage? Underestimation? Dependency?)
Review: What does the Schedule Management Plan say about recovery? Is there contingency? What does the Risk Register say (was this risk identified)?
Take Action: Determine options (fast-track, crash, re-scope). Present to sponsor with impact. Raise change request if baseline needs to change.
→ WRONG first action: "Immediately crash the critical path." (Skips assessment of WHY and what options exist)

Scenario 2: "A team member finds a serious defect in a module that was already accepted by the customer."
Assess: How serious is it? (Data loss? Security? Minor display issue?) Does it affect other modules? When was it introduced?
Review: Quality Management Plan — what is the defect severity classification? Was this defect covered by test cases?
Take Action: Classify severity, raise a defect repair change request, communicate to customer based on severity, implement fix, regression test.
→ WRONG first action: "Immediately tell the customer." (Before you understand the severity and have a fix ready, this creates panic)

Scenario 3: "The sponsor calls and says 'I heard the project is behind. Is it true?'"
Assess: Is it true? By how much? Do you have current data?
Review: The latest Work Performance Report and EVM data. What does the data actually say?
Take Action: Be honest. If behind: "Yes, we are 2 weeks behind due to X. Here is our recovery plan: [specific actions]. We will be back on track by [date]." If NOT behind: "Our current data shows SPI=1.02 — we are slightly ahead. Here is the source of that information."
→ WRONG action: "Tell the sponsor everything is fine" (dishonest if it is not, or confusing if you do not know yet)

Scenario 4: "A vendor says they cannot deliver by the contracted date."
Assess: How late? Why? Is this force majeure or vendor failure? What is the contract term? How does this affect the critical path?
Review: Procurement Management Plan and the contract itself. What are the remedies, cure periods, and escalation paths?
Take Action: Meet with the vendor face-to-face. Explore recovery options (expedite, partial delivery, substitute). If contractual breach — invoke remedies. Update risk register with new situation.

Scenario 5: "Two stakeholders are lobbying for contradictory features."
Assess: What are the actual requirements behind each position? Are they truly contradictory or just described differently?
Review: Requirements Traceability Matrix. Which requirements have higher priority? What does the scope baseline say?
Take Action: Facilitate a requirements workshop. Use MoSCoW or WSJF to objectively prioritise. If truly contradictory, escalate to sponsor for decision with options and trade-offs clearly presented.

Q A sponsor verbally approves a significant scope change and tells the PM to proceed immediately. What should the PM do?
✅ Answer: C
ALL scope changes require a formal change request through Integrated Change Control — including changes verbally approved by the sponsor. The CCB must formally review and approve. ✅ Answer: C

Configuration Management vs. Change Management

Configuration Management

  • Manages the specifications of deliverables and processes
  • Identifies which items are under configuration control
  • Tracks versions, status, and audit trail of configuration items
  • Example: Version 2.3 of the design document is the approved baseline

Change Management

  • Manages changes to baselines and project documents
  • Governs the process of raising, evaluating, approving changes
  • Change Control Board authority defined here
  • Example: A request to move from Version 2.3 to 2.4 must go through CCB

Project Management Information System (PMIS)

  • Automated system used to collect, store, distribute project information
  • Includes: scheduling software, work authorisation systems, configuration management systems, information collection/distribution systems
  • PMBOK 8: PMIS increasingly leverages AI for real-time tracking, analysis, and reporting
  • Common exam point: PMIS is a TOOL/TECHNIQUE, not an output. It is an EEF (part of the organisational infrastructure).

Q A quality inspection finds a defect in a delivered component. The PM wants to fix it. What type of change request is this?
✅ Answer: C
Fixing a nonconforming product component is a DEFECT REPAIR. Corrective action realigns project PERFORMANCE (schedule, cost) with the plan. Preventive action reduces probability of FUTURE problems. Update modifies documents. All four go through Integrated Change Control — defect repair is not exempt.
Q The PM identifies that a risky phase is approaching and wants to add extra testing before it begins to prevent problems. What type of change request is this?
✅ Answer: B
Adding a measure to REDUCE THE PROBABILITY of future problems is PREVENTIVE ACTION. It is proactive — no problem exists yet. Corrective action would apply if a problem already existed and needed correction. Preventive action still requires CCB approval because it modifies the plan (adds work/time/cost).
Q A stakeholder asks the PM: "Who has the authority to approve a change to the cost baseline?" Where should the PM look for the answer?
✅ Answer: C
The Change Management Plan (a component of the Project Management Plan) defines the change control process, including who has authority to approve which types of changes, CCB composition, and escalation thresholds. The Project Charter gives the PM general authority but not the specific change approval process. The Cost Management Plan defines how costs are managed, not who approves changes.
Q A project is at a phase gate. The steering committee reviews performance and finds the business case is no longer valid due to market changes. What is the appropriate decision?
✅ Answer: B
Phase gates exist precisely to make continue/modify/terminate decisions. If the business case is no longer valid, terminating is the correct choice — continuing wastes resources on a project that cannot deliver value. Sunk costs are irrelevant to forward-looking decisions (sunk cost fallacy). Reducing scope or pausing might be considered, but the question specifies the business case is INVALID — not diminished.
Q Which statement about the PMIS is correct?
✅ Answer: B
The PMIS is part of the organisational infrastructure — an Enterprise Environmental Factor (EEF). It is used as a tool/technique across many processes (scheduling, tracking, communication). It is not an output of any process. It exists at the organisational level and supports all project types — predictive, adaptive, and hybrid.

Real-World Example: Integrated Change Control in Practice

Scenario: You are PM for a ₹4 crore core banking system upgrade at a regional bank. Mid-execution, the CTO walks into your war room and says: "Our auditors need a new transaction audit trail module — add it to the project. We need it in 3 months."

WBS — Work Breakdown Structure

  • Definition: Hierarchical decomposition of total project scope into manageable work packages
  • 100% Rule: WBS must capture 100% of project scope — nothing more, nothing less
  • Work Package: Lowest-level WBS component — what gets estimated, scheduled, and assigned to a team
  • WBS Dictionary: Detailed description of each work package — owner, resources, duration, cost estimate, acceptance criteria
  • Planning Package: WBS component with known work content but not yet decomposed (used in rolling wave planning)
  • Scope Baseline = Project Scope Statement + WBS + WBS Dictionary (all three together)

Validate Scope vs. Control Scope — Frequently Confused

Validate Scope

  • Process of formalising ACCEPTANCE of completed deliverables
  • Done WITH the customer / sponsor / product owner
  • Input: Verified Deliverables (from Control Quality)
  • Output: Accepted Deliverables
  • Concerned with customer ACCEPTANCE
  • Performed in M&C Focus Area

Control Scope

  • Process of monitoring scope status and managing changes to scope baseline
  • Done by the project team continuously
  • Input: Work Performance Data
  • Output: Work Performance Information, Change Requests
  • Concerned with PREVENTING scope creep
  • Performed continuously throughout the project
🚨
Critical Sequence: Control Quality → Verified Deliverables → Validate Scope → Accepted Deliverables. Control Quality is ALWAYS performed BEFORE Validate Scope. This sequence is frequently tested.

Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM)

  • Links requirements from origin through to final deliverable — ensures nothing is missed
  • Traces: business need → business requirement → project requirement → design → test → delivery
  • Key exam point: If a requirement is not in the RTM, it is not in scope
  • Managed as part of Scope Management throughout the project lifecycle
Q A developer adds a visually appealing animation to a web app that was not in the approved requirements. The customer did not request it. This is:
✅ Answer: B
Gold plating is adding features beyond what was agreed in the scope baseline — even with good intentions. The developer decided (without authorisation) to add something extra. Gold plating wastes resources, introduces unreviewed risk, and may not align with customer priorities or technical constraints. Scope creep involves the customer or stakeholders requesting unauthorised additions.
Q Control Quality has been completed and Verified Deliverables produced. What is the NEXT step?
✅ Answer: B
After Control Quality produces Verified Deliverables, the next step is Validate Scope — where the customer or sponsor formally accepts the deliverables. Only Accepted Deliverables (the output of Validate Scope) can be transitioned to operations or used to close the project. You cannot close the project without formal acceptance.
Q Which document provides the detailed description of each WBS work package — including owner, estimated cost, duration, and acceptance criteria?
✅ Answer: C
The WBS Dictionary provides detailed descriptions of each component in the WBS, including: description, deliverables, accepting organisation, schedule milestones, associated schedule activities, resources required, cost estimates, quality requirements, acceptance criteria, technical references, and agreement information. Together with the Project Scope Statement and WBS, it forms the Scope Baseline.

Definition of Ready vs. Definition of Done

Definition of Ready (DoR)

  • Criteria a backlog item must meet BEFORE it can be pulled into a sprint
  • Example: story is clear, estimated, acceptance criteria defined, dependencies resolved
  • Prevents teams from starting work on poorly understood items
  • Not an official Scrum artifact but widely used

Definition of Done (DoD)

  • Criteria an item must meet to be considered COMPLETE
  • Example: code reviewed, tested, documented, deployed to staging
  • Ensures consistent quality across all increments
  • Official Scrum artifact commitment for the Increment
Q A PM wants to gather requirements from 200 end users across 5 countries. Which elicitation technique is MOST appropriate?
✅ Answer: C
For a large, geographically dispersed audience, questionnaires/surveys are most efficient. Interviews with 200 people would take months. A single workshop with 200 people across 5 countries is impractical. Observation of 200 users is not feasible. Surveys can reach all users efficiently and generate quantitative data.
Q Requirements gathering has stalled because a senior stakeholder dominates every meeting and others are afraid to disagree. Which technique should the PM use?
✅ Answer: B
The Delphi technique uses anonymous, iterative rounds of input — it specifically eliminates the influence of dominant personalities and groupthink. Participants provide opinions anonymously, a facilitator summarises, and rounds repeat until consensus. This is the textbook solution when a dominant stakeholder is suppressing input from others.
Q Using MoSCoW prioritisation, a product owner classifies a feature as "Could have." What does this mean?
✅ Answer: C
MoSCoW: MUST have = mandatory. SHOULD have = important but not critical. COULD have = desirable if time permits, minimal impact if excluded. WON'T have = explicitly excluded this time. "Could have" features are the first to be dropped if the sprint is running out of capacity.
Q A backlog item is pulled into a sprint but the team discovers midway that the acceptance criteria are unclear and a key dependency is unresolved. What should have prevented this?
✅ Answer: B
The Definition of Ready (DoR) defines what a backlog item must have BEFORE being pulled into a sprint — clear acceptance criteria, estimates, and resolved dependencies are typical DoR criteria. If the team had applied a DoR, this item would not have been accepted into the sprint until it was ready. DoD applies at the END (completion criteria), not the start.
Q Which technique would BEST help a product team identify features that would DELIGHT customers versus features customers merely expect?
✅ Answer: B
The Kano Model specifically categorises features into: Basic (expected — absence causes dissatisfaction, presence is neutral), Performance (more is better — linear satisfaction), and Excitement/Delighters (unexpected — presence creates delight, absence is neutral). It is the only technique designed to distinguish "expected" from "delighting" features.

Real-World Example: Scope Creep vs Gold Plating vs Legitimate Change

Project: Customer-facing payments portal for a fintech startup

Scenario A — Scope Creep:
The customer casually mentions in a meeting: "Oh, and can you add dark mode?" The developer adds it over the weekend without a change request.
→ This is SCOPE CREEP. Work was added without going through change control. Even if it takes only 4 hours — it sets a dangerous precedent, and now "dark mode" is in the product but not in any test plan, documentation, or baseline.

Scenario B — Gold Plating:
The developer decides to add animated micro-interactions to every button because "it will delight users." The customer never asked for this.
→ This is GOLD PLATING. The team is adding beyond agreed scope. It wastes time, adds untested features, and may not align with the customer's brand or performance requirements.

Scenario C — Legitimate Change:
The customer submits a written request for dark mode after seeing a competitor launch it. The PM raises a change request, assesses it (+3 days, +₹45,000), the CCB approves, and baselines are updated.
→ This is the RIGHT way. Change is welcome — uncontrolled change is the problem.

The rule: Any addition to scope, however small, requires a change request. "Small" changes are where scope creep enters.

Requirements Elicitation — Choosing the Right Technique

Situation 1: You are gathering requirements for a new core banking system that will be used by 2,000 branch staff across India. Most cannot be interviewed individually.
→ Best technique: Survey/Questionnaire — reach large, dispersed audience efficiently. Follow up with Focus Groups in 3–4 regional hubs for depth.

Critical Path Method (CPM)

  • Critical Path: The LONGEST path through the network diagram — determines the earliest possible project completion date
  • Total Float/Slack: Amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the project end date
  • Critical Path activities = ZERO total float — any delay to them delays the entire project
  • Free Float: Time an activity can delay without delaying its immediate SUCCESSOR (may be different from total float)
  • Negative Float: Project is already behind schedule — critical path activities are late
  • Near-critical path: Paths with very low float — monitor closely as they could become critical
🔑
If a question asks which activities to prioritise — ALWAYS prioritise critical path activities (zero float) first. Non-critical activities have slack that can absorb minor delays.

Lead and Lag

Lead (Negative Lag — Overlap)

  • Activity B starts BEFORE Activity A is complete
  • Creates an overlap between activities
  • Shown as negative lag in scheduling tools
  • REDUCES overall project duration
  • Example: Landscaping starts 2 weeks before punch list completion (FS –2 weeks)

Lag (Waiting Time — Delay)

  • A mandatory wait BETWEEN activities
  • Successor must wait after predecessor starts or finishes
  • EXTENDS overall project duration
  • Duration estimates do NOT include lead or lag time
  • Example: Editing starts 15 days after writing begins (SS +15 days)

Rolling Wave Planning

  • Near-term work is planned in detail; far future work is planned at a higher level
  • Detail is added progressively as the project moves forward and more information becomes available
  • NOT a sign of poor planning — it is deliberately embraced in uncertain environments
  • Used in both predictive (for future phases) and adaptive (sprint-by-sprint) approaches
  • Also called Progressive Elaboration when referring to the planning process itself
Q Activity A has total float of 0 days. Activity B has total float of 5 days. Which statement is correct?
✅ Answer: C
Critical path activities have ZERO total float — any delay to them delays the project end date. Activity A (0 float) is on the critical path. Activity B (5 days float) can be delayed up to 5 days without impacting the project end date — it is NOT on the critical path. The PM should focus attention on Activity A.
Q A project must be shortened by 2 weeks. The sponsor says no additional budget is available. Which technique should the PM use?
✅ Answer: C
Fast Tracking performs activities in parallel that were planned sequentially. It does NOT add direct cost — making it the right choice when budget cannot be increased. The risk is increased rework if earlier work needs to change while later work is already underway. Crashing would require additional budget. Resource leveling typically EXTENDS the schedule.
Q Using PERT estimation with O=10, M=15, P=20, what is the estimated duration and standard deviation?
✅ Answer: A
PERT = (O + 4M + P)/6 = (10 + 60 + 20)/6 = 90/6 = 15 days. Standard Deviation σ = (P − O)/6 = (20 − 10)/6 = 10/6 = 1.67 days. When O, M, and P are symmetric around the most likely value, PERT equals the most likely estimate (15 days in this case).

Forward PassES = max(EF of all predecessors). EF = ES + Duration − 1 (or ES + Duration if using 0-based)Early Start (ES) and Early Finish (EF) for every activity
Backward PassLF = min(LS of all successors). LS = LF − Duration + 1Late Start (LS) and Late Finish (LF) for every activity

B: ES=3, EF=7  |  C: ES=3, EF=9
D: ES = max(7, 9) = 9, EF = 11

Q A construction project must pour the foundation before erecting the steel frame. This dependency is:

Q A PM needs to compress the schedule and identifies that Design (mandatory dependency to Development) and Documentation (discretionary dependency to Development) both precede Development. Which can be fast-tracked?
✅ Answer: B
Fast-tracking overlaps activities. Only DISCRETIONARY dependencies can be safely overlapped because they are preferences, not requirements. Fast-tracking mandatory dependencies causes rework or failure.

✅ Answer: B
In a burnup chart, the scope line represents total work in the release. When it jumps upward, scope was ADDED (new stories accepted into the release). The completed line continuing at its steady slope means velocity was unchanged. This is exactly what burnup charts are designed to reveal — scope changes that burndown charts hide.

Crashing vs Fast Tracking — Decision Framework

Scenario: Your 20-day project needs to be done in 16 days. Critical path: A(4)→B(6)→C(5)→D(5). Non-critical path: A(4)→E(3)→D(5) — total 12 days with 8 days float.

Option 1: Fast Track
Can C and D overlap? If C→D dependency is discretionary (C should finish before D starts, but not mandatory): Start D when C is 50% complete (3 days). New duration for C+D: 3+5=8 days instead of 10. Project: 4+6+8=18 days. Saved 2 days, no cost.
Can B and C overlap? B→C is mandatory (design must be done). Cannot fast-track.
Result: Fast tracking C/D gives 18 days — need 2 more days saved.

Option 2: Crash B
B is 6 days. Adding 2 resources reduces it to 4 days at extra cost of ₹50,000.
New duration: 4+4+8=16 days. ✅ Target met.
Total cost increase: ₹50,000.

PM Decision Framework:
1. Try fast tracking first (free) on discretionary dependencies only
2. If not enough, crash the critical path activity with the LOWEST cost per day saved
3. Only crash activities ON the critical path — crashing non-critical activities wastes money
4. After each crash, recalculate critical path — another path may become critical

Schedule Compression Decision Tree

Project is behind schedule. Apply this sequence:

Step 1: Which activities are on the critical path? (Only these matter for compression)

Step 2: Can any critical path activities be fast-tracked (overlapped)?
→ YES: Check if dependency is DISCRETIONARY. If yes, overlap by 25–50%. Monitor rework risk.
→ NO: All dependencies are mandatory. Go to Step 3.

Step 3: Can any critical path activities be crashed (resources added)?
→ YES: Calculate crash cost per day for each critical activity. Crash the CHEAPEST first.
Example: Activity B costs ₹10,000/day to crash. Activity C costs ₹25,000/day. Crash B first.
→ NO: Cannot add resources. Go to Step 4.

Step 4: Can scope be reduced (remove low-priority requirements)?
→ Requires formal change request. Sponsor/customer must agree.

Step 5: Escalate to sponsor with options and cost. Sponsor decides.

NEVER: Cut quality testing, hide the delay, or work the team unsustainable hours without addressing the root cause.

Reserve Analysis

Contingency Reserve

  • For known unknowns (identified risks in the risk register)
  • Managed by the PM — within PM's authority
  • Part of the project cost baseline
  • Triggered when a risk response plan is activated
  • Shown in cost baseline and schedule baseline

Management Reserve

  • For unknown unknowns (unforeseeable risks)
  • Controlled by management/sponsor — NOT the PM
  • NOT part of PM's baseline — separate from baseline
  • Requires management approval to access
  • Not included in Earned Value calculations
⚠️
If a PM uses management reserve without approval — that is a problem. If a PM uses contingency reserve for an identified risk — that is correct. Know the difference.

Q EV = $60,000 | AC = $80,000 | PV = $70,000 | BAC = $150,000. What is the CPI and what does it mean?
✅ Answer: A
CPI = EV / AC = 60,000 / 80,000 = 0.75. A CPI less than 1.0 means OVER BUDGET — for every $1 spent, only $0.75 of value is produced. This is a cost efficiency issue. The SPI = EV/PV = 60,000/70,000 = 0.857, meaning also behind schedule. Both metrics are negative.
Q Using the same figures (EV=$60K, AC=$80K, BAC=$150K, CPI=0.75), what is the Estimate at Completion (assuming current CPI continues)?
✅ Answer: C
EAC (typical) = BAC / CPI = 150,000 / 0.75 = $200,000. This assumes the project continues at the current cost efficiency (CPI = 0.75) for the remainder. The project is forecast to cost $50,000 more than planned (VAC = 150,000 − 200,000 = −$50,000).
Q A project has BAC=$500K, EV=$200K, AC=$180K. The TCPI needed to meet the BAC is calculated as:
✅ Answer: A
TCPI (to BAC) = (BAC − EV) / (BAC − AC) = (500,000 − 200,000) / (500,000 − 180,000) = 300,000 / 320,000 = 0.9375. A TCPI less than 1.0 means the remaining work needs to be done at LESS efficiency than currently — the project can afford to be slightly less efficient and still meet the BAC. This is a relatively good position (CPI = 200,000/180,000 = 1.11 — already under budget).

Q A project has BAC=$400K, EV=$160K, AC=$200K. The PM notes that cost overruns to date were caused by a one-time equipment failure that has been resolved and will not recur. What is the EAC?
✅ Answer: B
The variance is ATYPICAL (one-time, will not recur), so use: EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) = 200,000 + (400,000 − 160,000) = 200,000 + 240,000 = $440,000. Remaining work will be done at the PLANNED rate, not the current (poor) CPI. If you used BAC/CPI = 400,000/0.8 = $500,000 — that assumes the overrun continues, which the scenario explicitly says it won't.
Q Two projects are being compared. Project A: NPV = $500K, payback 3 years. Project B: NPV = $300K, payback 1.5 years. Using PMI's preferred selection criterion, which should be chosen?
✅ Answer: B
PMI prefers NPV as the primary project selection criterion because it accounts for time value of money and total value created. Project A has $200K higher NPV. Payback period is a secondary consideration — it ignores time value of money and cash flows after payback. Unless the question specifies a cash-flow constraint, NPV wins.
Q A PM proposes continuing a struggling project because "we have already invested $2M and cannot let that go to waste." What decision-making error is this?
✅ Answer: B
The Sunk Cost Fallacy — allowing already-spent, unrecoverable money to influence forward-looking decisions. The $2M is gone regardless of whether the project continues. The only relevant question is: will FUTURE investment generate sufficient FUTURE value? PMI expects PMs to make decisions based on expected future value, not past spending. (Escalation of commitment is closely related but the specific reasoning here is sunk cost.)
Q Which component is NOT included in the cost baseline?
✅ Answer: C
The Cost Baseline = activity estimates + work package costs + contingency reserves. Management Reserve is added ON TOP of the cost baseline to form the Project Budget — it is NOT part of the baseline. EVM measures against the baseline, so management reserve is excluded from EV calculations. This is one of the most common exam traps in Cost Management.
Q A project has a BCR of 0.85. What does this mean for the go/no-go decision?
✅ Answer: B
BCR = PV of Benefits ÷ PV of Costs. A BCR of 0.85 means benefits are only 85% of costs — for every $1 invested, only $0.85 of benefit is returned. BCR < 1 means costs EXCEED benefits — the project should not proceed on financial grounds. BCR > 1 is required for a financially sound project. BCR is not the same as ROI.

Quality Philosophy

  • Prevention over inspection — build quality in from the start; it is always cheaper to prevent defects than to find and fix them
  • Customer satisfaction — meet or exceed stated AND unstated customer expectations. Fitness for use.
  • Continuous improvement (Kaizen) — always seek ways to improve processes and outputs; never stop improving
  • Cost of Quality: Money spent on achieving quality AND money lost from lack of quality — both must be managed
  • Quality is PLANNED in — it is not added at the end through testing and inspection alone

Histogram

Flowchart
Maps PROCESS STEPS and decision points. Helps understand where defects can enter and identifies improvement opportunities.
Check Sheet
Structured form for COLLECTING and tallying data in real time. Simple tally sheet counting occurrences of defects or problems.

Q During quality audits, it is determined that the team is not following agreed-upon processes. Which quality process does a quality audit belong to?
✅ Answer: C
Quality audits are a key tool of Manage Quality (Executing process). Audits examine whether quality PROCESSES are being followed correctly — they look at HOW the work is being done. Control Quality inspects the actual DELIVERABLES/OUTPUTS. Manage Quality focuses on the quality of processes and procedures.
Q Seven consecutive data points appear on the same side of the control chart mean, but all are within control limits. What should the PM conclude?
✅ Answer: B
The Rule of Seven states that seven or more consecutive data points on the same side of the mean indicate the process is OUT OF CONTROL — even if all points are within the control limits. This non-random pattern signals an assignable (identifiable) cause that must be investigated and corrected. A truly random process would not produce 7 consecutive points on one side.
Q A customer complaint is received after delivery, requiring a product recall and warranty replacement. Which cost of quality category is this?
✅ Answer: D
External Failure Costs occur AFTER the product has been delivered to the customer — warranty claims, recalls, customer complaints, liability. These are the most expensive type of quality failure. Internal failure costs (rework, scrap) occur before delivery. Prevention and appraisal costs are conformance costs that try to avoid failures.

Quality in Agile

  • Definition of Done: Team-agreed quality criteria every increment must meet
  • Test-Driven Development (TDD): Write tests first, then code to pass the tests
  • Continuous Integration: Code merged and tested frequently — catches defects early
  • Pair Programming: Two developers; one codes, one reviews in real time
  • Refactoring: Improving code structure without changing behaviour
  • Retrospectives: Regular process quality improvement
  • Technical Debt: Shortcuts taken that must be "repaid" later — should be tracked and managed
Q A budget smartphone with basic features works perfectly and reliably. A premium smartphone with advanced features crashes frequently. Which statement is correct?
✅ Answer: B
GRADE = category based on features (budget = low grade, premium = high grade). QUALITY = degree to which the product meets requirements and works as intended. The budget phone WORKS (high quality, low grade). The premium phone CRASHES (low quality, high grade). Low grade is acceptable — it is a design choice. Low quality is NEVER acceptable — it is a defect.
Q A measuring instrument consistently reads 2mm higher than the true value across all measurements. The instrument is:
✅ Answer: B
PRECISE = consistent, repeatable measurements (tight cluster). ACCURATE = close to the true value. The instrument gives CONSISTENT readings (precise) but they are all WRONG by the same amount (not accurate). This is systematic bias — precision without accuracy. A calibration would fix accuracy while maintaining precision.
Q Which quality guru is associated with the concept "Quality is Free" and Zero Defects?
✅ Answer: C
Philip Crosby wrote "Quality is Free" and championed Zero Defects. His argument: the cost of preventing defects is always less than the cost of fixing them — so quality effectively pays for itself. Deming is known for PDCA and 14 Points. Juran for the Quality Trilogy and "fitness for use." Ishikawa for the fishbone diagram and quality circles.
Q A software team consistently takes shortcuts to meet sprint deadlines, accumulating code that "works but is messy." What should the Scrum Master ensure happens?
✅ Answer: B
Technical debt (shortcuts that must be repaid later) is a real agile concept. It should be TRACKED visibly (in the backlog) and ADDRESSED through allocated capacity in sprints — not ignored, and not by stopping all feature delivery. The Scrum Master coaches the team and PO on balancing new features with technical debt reduction. Reporting to management violates servant leadership.
Q During Control Quality, an inspector uses a sample of 50 units out of 5,000 to determine if the batch meets specifications. This is an example of:
✅ Answer: C
Statistical sampling = inspecting a representative subset (50 of 5,000) rather than 100% of units, to draw conclusions about the whole population. It reduces the cost of quality (appraisal cost) while providing adequate confidence. Attribute vs Variable sampling describes HOW each unit is measured (pass/fail vs continuous scale). DOE is for identifying influential variables in design.

Control Chart — Reading in Real Life

Process: Daily transaction processing error rate (%) over 30 days
Mean: 1.2% error rate
UCL: 2.8% (Upper Control Limit = mean + 3σ)
LCL: 0.0% (Lower Control Limit — cannot be negative for a rate)

Day 15–21: Seven consecutive points all ABOVE the mean (1.3%, 1.5%, 1.7%, 1.8%, 1.9%, 2.0%, 2.1%) — all still within UCL

→ Out of control — assignable cause. Investigation: a system update introduced a bug.
→ Action: rollback the update; raise a defect repair change request.

Key insight: The Rule of Seven caught the training issue BEFORE it became a limit violation. This is why you watch for PATTERNS, not just limit breaches.

Opportunity: EMV is POSITIVE (potential gain)
Example (threat): 30% chance of $100,000 loss → EMV = −$30,000
Example (opportunity): 40% chance of $50,000 gain → EMV = +$20,000
Decision Tree: Sum the EMVs of each branch to find best decision path

Q A project team decides to purchase insurance to cover potential damage to equipment on site. Which risk response strategy is this?
✅ Answer: C
Purchasing insurance is a Transfer strategy — the financial consequence of the risk (equipment damage) is shifted to the insurance company (third party). Transfer does NOT eliminate the risk — it shifts the monetary impact. The risk of physical damage still exists; only the financial burden is transferred.
Q After implementing a risk mitigation strategy, the team identifies a new risk that emerged as a direct result of the mitigation actions. This new risk is called a:
✅ Answer: B
A Secondary Risk is a new risk that arises as a DIRECT RESULT of implementing a risk response. Both secondary and residual risks must be identified, analyzed, and responded to. Residual risk is what remains after the response — not something NEW created by it. A workaround is an unplanned response to an unidentified risk that occurs.
Q A project has two risk response options. Option A: 70% probability of $200,000 savings. Option B: 90% probability of $100,000 savings. Using EMV, which option should the PM recommend?
✅ Answer: A
EMV (Option A) = 70% × $200,000 = $140,000. EMV (Option B) = 90% × $100,000 = $90,000. Option A has a higher Expected Monetary Value ($140K vs $90K), making it the better choice from a risk analysis perspective. PMI expects you to use quantitative analysis (EMV) to make objective decisions, not just choose the "safer" or "higher potential" option.

Decision Tree Analysis — Worked Example

Scenario: Build a new facility or upgrade the existing one?

Option 1: Build New — Cost $200K
  60% chance of high demand → revenue $500K
  40% chance of low demand → revenue $200K
  EMV = (0.6 × 500K) + (0.4 × 200K) − 200K = 300K + 80K − 200K = +$180K

Option 2: Upgrade Existing — Cost $80K
  60% chance of high demand → revenue $300K
  40% chance of low demand → revenue $150K
  EMV = (0.6 × 300K) + (0.4 × 150K) − 80K = 180K + 60K − 80K = +$160K

Decision: Build New has higher EMV (+$180K vs +$160K). Choose Build New.

Monte Carlo Simulation

  • Runs thousands of iterations using random values from probability distributions for each activity
  • Output: probability distribution of project completion date or cost (e.g., "80% confident of finishing by June 15")
  • Reveals which activities are most likely to end up on the critical path (criticality index)
  • Used in quantitative risk analysis for large/complex projects
  • Result is an S-curve showing cumulative probability

Q A team uses a checklist based on lessons learned from five previous similar projects to identify risks. What is the primary LIMITATION of this technique?
✅ Answer: B
Checklists are efficient but limited to historically known risks. They may miss NEW risks unique to this project's context, technology, or environment. PMI recommends using checklists as a STARTING point supplemented by brainstorming, interviews, and other techniques. Checklists can include positive risks. They are quick, not time-consuming.
Q A risk has probability 2/5 and impact 5/5 (catastrophic). Another risk has probability 5/5 and impact 2/5. Both have the same P×I score. How should the PM treat them?
✅ Answer: B
While P×I scores are equal, a CATASTROPHIC impact (even at lower probability) typically warrants more attention — the consequence of occurrence could be project-ending. PMI expects PMs to apply judgment beyond raw scores. The high-probability low-impact risk can often be accepted or handled with simple mitigation. The catastrophic risk needs a contingency plan.
Q A team is uncertain whether a new database technology will perform adequately. Rather than committing the whole sprint, they allocate 2 days to build a proof of concept. This is called a:
✅ Answer: B
A SPIKE is a time-boxed investigation or research activity in agile — used to reduce uncertainty (technical or functional) before committing to a full implementation. It is a risk reduction technique specific to agile. The output is knowledge, not a shippable increment. A prototype is a working model; Sprint 0 is a preparatory sprint; technical debt is accumulated shortcuts.
Q Monte Carlo simulation shows a 50% probability of finishing by March 15 and 90% probability by April 20. The sponsor wants a committed date. What should the PM communicate?
✅ Answer: B
For a COMMITTED date, a high confidence level (typically 80–90%) is appropriate. March 15 has only 50% confidence — a coin flip. April 20 has 90% confidence — a reliable commitment. PMI expects PMs to communicate commitments at appropriate confidence levels. Refusing to commit is not helpful; the simulation exists precisely to enable informed commitments.
Q Which risk identification technique specifically helps identify BOTH internal strengths/weaknesses AND external opportunities/threats?
✅ Answer: B
SWOT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) analysis examines both INTERNAL factors (Strengths and Weaknesses of the organisation/project) and EXTERNAL factors (Opportunities and Threats from the environment). It is one of the few techniques explicitly designed to surface both positive risks (opportunities) and negative risks (threats).

Decision Tree — Make or Buy Analysis

Decision: Should we build the payment reconciliation module in-house or buy a vendor solution?

Option A: Build In-House — Cost: ₹25L
• 70% chance project team can deliver on time → Revenue benefit: ₹80L → Net: ₹80L−₹25L=₹55L
• 30% chance of delays (6 months) → Revenue benefit delayed: ₹50L → Net: ₹50L−₹25L=₹25L
EMV(Build) = (0.70×₹55L) + (0.30×₹25L) = ₹38.5L + ₹7.5L = ₹46L

Option B: Buy Vendor Solution — Cost: ₹18L
• 90% chance vendor delivers as promised → Benefit: ₹75L → Net: ₹75L−₹18L=₹57L
• 10% chance vendor delays (integration issues) → Benefit: ₹55L → Net: ₹55L−₹18L=₹37L
EMV(Buy) = (0.90×₹57L) + (0.10×₹37L) = ₹51.3L + ₹3.7L = ₹55L

• Does building give us a strategic asset (IP, capability)? → May favour Build
• Do we have the team capacity to build? → May favour Buy
• Is the vendor financially stable? → Risk of Buy option

✅ Answer: C
A resistant stakeholder needs targeted, personal engagement — not reduced involvement (which increases their negative influence) or escalation (premature). The PM should meet face-to-face, understand their specific concerns, involve them in relevant decisions, and work proactively to move them toward a neutral or supportive position. Q A PM sends a detailed email about a critical decision. The recipient later says they never understood the email. Who bears primary responsibility for this communication failure?
✅ Answer: B
In PMI's communication model, the SENDER is responsible for ensuring the message is clearly encoded AND confirming it was understood (seeking feedback/acknowledgement). For a CRITICAL decision, the PM should have used interactive communication (meeting/call) or at minimum followed up to confirm understanding. The receiver has a role, but the sender initiated and bears primary responsibility.

Communication Channels — Why This Formula Matters

Why does communication complexity matter on real projects?

Small team (5 people): 5×4/2 = 10 channels. A PM can personally manage all relationships. Daily standup covers all coordination needs.

Medium team (15 people): 15×14/2 = 105 channels. The PM cannot personally manage 105 relationships. Communication breaks down, assumptions form, decisions are made in silos. Need: team lead structure, documented decisions, clear escalation paths.

Large project (50 stakeholders): 50×49/2 = 1,225 channels. Without a formal Communications Management Plan, the project will drown in emails, conflicting information, and missed stakeholders.

Real impact: Research shows 56% of project budget risk comes from poor communication (PMI Pulse of the Profession). The channel formula makes this concrete — it is not a theoretical formula, it is a communication complexity warning system.

Exam trap: "Adding 3 people to a 12-person team creates how many NEW channels?"
Before: 12×11/2 = 66. After: 15×14/2 = 105. New channels = 105−66 = 39 new channels.
Not 3. Not 36. 39. The complexity grows non-linearly.

Point of Total Assumption (PTA) — FPIF Contracts Only

Point of Total Assumption
PTA = ((Ceiling Price − Target Price) / Buyer's Share Ratio) + Target Cost
PTA = cost point beyond which the SELLER bears ALL overruns (acts like a fixed-price contract)
If actual costs exceed PTA: seller loses money on every additional dollar spent
Example: Target Cost = $100K, Target Price = $110K, Ceiling = $130K, Buyer Share = 80%
PTA = ((130,000 − 110,000) / 0.80) + 100,000 = 25,000 + 100,000 = $125,000

Q A project has highly uncertain and evolving scope requirements. The PM recommends a contract type. Which contract type BEST protects the buyer from overpaying for uncertain scope?
✅ Answer: B
With UNCERTAIN scope, a Cost Plus Fixed Fee (CPFF) contract is most appropriate. With FFP, the seller adds large contingency buffers for uncertainty (making it expensive) and may cut corners to stay within price. CPFF pays the seller's actual costs + a fixed fee — the buyer pays actual costs but gets transparent, quality work without the seller gaming the scope uncertainty. T&M is also reasonable but less structured.
Q In an FPIF contract: Target Cost = $200K, Target Price = $220K, Ceiling Price = $260K, Buyer Share Ratio = 80%. What is the Point of Total Assumption (PTA)?
✅ Answer: B
PTA = ((Ceiling Price − Target Price) / Buyer Share Ratio) + Target Cost = ((260,000 − 220,000) / 0.80) + 200,000 = (40,000 / 0.80) + 200,000 = 50,000 + 200,000 = $250,000. Above $250K actual cost, the seller bears ALL overruns — the contract behaves like a fixed-price contract at that point.
Q A vendor claims the project team caused scope changes that increased their costs, leading to a dispute. What should the PM do FIRST?
✅ Answer: C
A.R.T.: First, ASSESS the situation — review the procurement contract, SOW, change logs, and communications to understand what was actually agreed and what changes occurred. This factual foundation is needed before any action. If a genuine contractual dispute exists after review, escalate to Claims Administration (not senior management). Termination is a last resort. Paying without review sets a bad precedent.

Q During a bidder conference, one seller privately asks the PM a question about a technical requirement. What should the PM do?
✅ Answer: B

The PM should answer the question but share BOTH the question and answer with all bidders (typically via addendum to the RFP). Answering privately gives one bidder an unfair advantage. Q A buyer verbally instructs the seller to add a feature not in the contract. The seller complies. Later, the buyer refuses to pay for the extra work. What is this situation called?
✅ Answer: B
A CONSTRUCTIVE CHANGE occurs when the buyer directs (even informally) work outside the contract scope without a formal change order. The seller performed the work in good faith and has a legitimate claim for compensation. This is why ALL contract changes must go through the formal contract change control process — to protect both parties.
Q Which of the following is NOT typically one of the essential elements of a legally binding contract?
✅ Answer: C
The essential elements of a contract are: offer, acceptance, consideration, legal capacity, and legal purpose. A detailed WBS is a project management artifact — not a legal requirement for contract validity. Contracts may reference a statement of work, but a WBS is not a legal prerequisite.
Q An agile project needs to procure software development services. Requirements will evolve sprint by sprint. Which contracting approach is MOST appropriate?
✅ Answer: B
Agile procurement uses a Master Services Agreement (MSA) establishing terms, with lighter-weight statements of work per sprint or release. T&M (or capped T&M) pricing accommodates evolving scope. Fixed price contracts penalise change — contrary to agile values. A detailed 12-month plan contradicts adaptive planning. Penalty clauses for scope changes are incompatible with "responding to change."
Q A seller and buyer disagree about whether a delivered component meets the contract specifications. The seller demands payment; the buyer refuses. What is the FIRST step?
✅ Answer: C
Claims (disagreements about contract terms or compensation) should be resolved through Claims Administration — starting with NEGOTIATION between the parties. If negotiation fails: mediation → arbitration → litigation (as a last resort). Termination and lawsuits are extreme first steps. The sponsor does not have contractual authority to resolve a claim — that follows the contract's dispute resolution process.

📖 Worked Example: Choosing the Right Contract Type

Project: A fintech startup needs a mobile payments SDK. Three possible scenarios:

Scenario A — Requirements are crystal clear:
The startup needs an off-the-shelf Aadhaar eKYC API integration. The spec is fully defined, the vendor has done it 50 times, and pricing is standard.
→ Use Firm Fixed Price (FFP). Scope is defined, risk to seller is low, price is fair. Buyer's cost is locked. No surprises.

Scenario B — Requirements will evolve:
The startup wants a custom AI fraud detection engine. The exact features depend on what the ML models reveal during development. Nobody knows the final scope.
→ Use Cost Plus Fixed Fee (CPFF) or T&M with a cap. Forcing a fixed price here means the vendor either overcharges (big contingency) or cuts corners. Pay actual costs + a fixed fee.

Scenario C — Defined scope, want to motivate the vendor to be efficient:
A core banking migration with a well-defined scope, but a 12-month timeline where the startup wants the vendor to finish early and under budget.
→ Use Fixed Price Incentive Fee (FPIF). Target cost agreed. If vendor beats it, both share the savings. If vendor exceeds it, both share the pain (up to the Ceiling Price).

PTA Calculation (FPIF):
Target Cost = ₹80L | Target Price = ₹88L | Ceiling = ₹1.10Cr | Buyer Share = 80%
PTA = ((1.10Cr − 88L) / 0.80) + 80L = (22L / 0.80) + 80L = 27.5L + 80L = ₹1.075 Cr
Above ₹1.075 Cr actual cost → vendor absorbs ALL overruns. Acts like FFP.

📖 Worked Example: Vendor Dispute — Step by Step

Situation: A software vendor claims they delivered Phase 2 of a banking app and demands ₹45L payment. Your team says Phase 2 deliverables are incomplete — the reconciliation module is missing. The vendor says reconciliation was out of scope.

What the PM does:

Step 1 — Review the contract and SOW first (not the vendor's invoice).
Pull out the Statement of Work. Find the Phase 2 deliverables list. Does "reconciliation module" appear?

Step 2 — Fact-finding with your team.
Ask: Was reconciliation discussed in any email, meeting note, or change request? Is there any written record it was excluded?

Step 3 — Attempt negotiation face-to-face.
"Our SOW Section 3.2 lists reconciliation as a Phase 2 deliverable. Here is the clause. Can you show me where it was excluded?" Use data, not emotion.

Step 4 — If unresolved → Claims Administration.
Formally raise it as a claim. Follow the dispute resolution path in the contract: Negotiation → Mediation → Arbitration → (last resort) Litigation.

Step 5 — Document everything.
Every conversation, every email, every reference to the contract clause — timestamped and filed.

What the PM does NOT do:
• Pay immediately to "preserve the relationship"
• Terminate the contract without following the dispute process
• Let legal teams fight it without PM involvement in the fact-finding

📖 Worked Example: Bidder Conference

Project: Procuring a fraud detection AI platform for a Tier-2 bank.

Before the conference:
RFP issued to 6 shortlisted vendors. Vendor 3 emails the PM privately: "Can you clarify what 'real-time' means in your latency requirement? We need to know if <200ms is acceptable."

What the PM does (correct):
Does NOT reply privately. Instead:
1. Notes the question for the bidder conference
2. At the conference, reads the question (anonymously): "One vendor has asked what 'real-time' means — specifically whether sub-200ms latency is acceptable."
3. PM and technical lead answer: "Real-time means <500ms for transaction scoring; <200ms is preferred but not mandatory."
4. After the conference, sends written Q&A to ALL 6 vendors as Addendum 1 to the RFP.

Why this matters:
Every vendor now has the same information. This is PMI Fairness — equal access to information for all qualified candidates. If Vendor 3 had received a private answer and proposed a tighter SLA, they would have an unfair competitive advantage.

Exam trigger: Any time a question mentions a vendor asking privately, or the PM sharing information with one vendor — the answer always involves sharing with ALL vendors or routing through the formal conference process.

Q A newly hired developer is enthusiastic but has never used the project's technology stack. According to Situational Leadership, which style should the PM use?
✅ Answer: B

Q A team frequently argues about whether decisions should be made by the PM alone or by the whole team. What should the PM do?
✅ Answer: B
This is a team norms issue — the Team Charter should define how decisions are made (which decisions are PM's, which are team consensus, which are PO's). The team creates the charter together, which builds buy-in. Asserting authority violates servant leadership. Escalating abdicates PM responsibility. Voting each time is inefficient and does not resolve the underlying ambiguity.
Q The PM wants to reward a team member who worked 60-hour weeks to meet a deadline. What is the PMI concern with this approach?
✅ Answer: B
Rewarding overtime sends the message that working excessive hours is valued — encouraging burnout and an unsustainable pace. PMI's resource management guidance says reward DESIRABLE behaviours. The better approach: address WHY 60-hour weeks were needed (poor planning? overcommitment?) and reward efficiency, quality, and collaboration instead. Sustainable pace is an agile principle too.
Q Which statement correctly distinguishes coaching from mentoring?
✅ Answer: B
COACHING: goal-focused performance improvement; the coach asks questions to help the person find their own answers; typically medium-term. MENTORING: long-term career development; the mentor shares their own experience and wisdom; relationship-based. Both can be done by anyone with the relevant experience — not tied to management levels or seniority.
Q An agile team uses "Fist of Five" to check agreement on a proposed approach. Three members show 4 fingers, one shows 2 fingers. What should happen?
✅ Answer: B
Fist of Five is a CONSENSUS technique. A 2 (or lower) typically means "I have significant concerns." Consensus means everyone can live with the decision — a 2 signals they cannot yet. The correct step is to hear and address the concerns of the person showing 2, then re-check. Proceeding on majority ignores consensus. Repeated voting without discussion is coercive. PM deciding violates the team's autonomy.

Tuckman Stages — What They Actually Look Like

Project: New payments compliance project. Team of 8 assembled from across the organisation.

WEEK 1–2 — FORMING:
Team is polite but cautious. Nobody disagrees openly. Attendance at meetings is perfect. People look to the PM for every decision. Nobody volunteers opinions on the technical approach.

A heated debate breaks out between the backend developer and the QA lead about test coverage requirements. The developer says "we don't have time for 90% coverage." The QA lead says "anything less is a regulatory risk." Two team members stop talking directly and communicate only through the PM. Sprint velocity drops 30%.
→ PM action: Meet individually with both parties. Understand each perspective. Bring them together with a shared goal ("what does 'good enough' coverage mean for this specific regulation?"). Reference the quality plan. Facilitate a decision.

WEEK 6–8 — NORMING:
The team agrees on a tiered testing approach: 90% for payment logic, 70% for reporting. Norms establish: decisions made by the team, not routed through PM. Velocity recovers. Team members start helping each other without being asked.

WEEK 9–20 — PERFORMING:
Team self-organises daily standups without PM facilitation. Retrospectives generate concrete improvements. Velocity is 15% above initial baseline. Team catches a regulatory interpretation error before it becomes a defect.

Project completes. Several team members are quiet. The developer who fought with the QA lead says "I'll miss this team." PM organises a celebration, writes personal notes of thanks to each member, and ensures lessons are captured before the team disperses.

A developer in your remote team in Pune mentions that the company has not processed his expenses from last month and he is struggling financially.

SITUATION 2 — Herzberg applied:
You give the team a 15% salary increase. Two weeks later, velocity is the same. A team member says "I still feel like my work doesn't matter."
→ The salary addressed a hygiene factor (prevented dissatisfaction) but did not create motivation. The team member needs a MOTIVATOR — meaningful work, recognition, growth opportunity. PM action: assign them to a high-impact module and acknowledge their expertise publicly.

SITUATION 3 — McClelland applied:
You have two senior developers with equal skills. Dev A loves technical challenges, works alone best, gets frustrated in meetings. Dev B is a natural mentor, energised by collaboration, volunteers to lead the interns.
→ Dev A has high Achievement need → assign complex solo research spikes
→ Dev B has high Affiliation need → make them the team's knowledge-sharing lead, pair with juniors

SITUATION 4 — Pink's model applied:
A talented product analyst keeps arriving late and missing deadlines despite having market-rate pay, a good manager, and interesting work.
→ Check for AUTONOMY: Does she control HOW she works, or is every task micro-managed?
→ Check for MASTERY: Is she learning, or doing the same thing repeatedly?
→ Check for PURPOSE: Does she see how her work connects to the product's impact?
PM action: have a 1:1 to explore what's missing. The answer is usually in one of these three areas.

Ethics — Applying the EDMF Framework in Real Scenarios

Scenario: You are PM on a healthcare data migration project. A vendor account manager takes you to an expensive dinner (₹15,000/person) during the evaluation phase. At dinner, he hints that his company can "adjust the proposal" if needed.

Apply EDMF:

Step 1 — Assessment:
Facts: You are in vendor evaluation. This is an expensive gift/entertainment. There is a hint of proposal flexibility. This may constitute bribery. Affected: your organisation (fairness of selection), other vendors (unfair advantage), patients (if wrong vendor selected).

Step 2 — Alternatives:
A) Accept dinner and continue normally
B) Accept dinner, declare it, and recuse yourself from evaluation
C) Decline the dinner
D) Decline, document the interaction, and report to your manager

Step 3 — Analysis:
A violates Fairness (conflict of interest) and Honesty (the hint was a corruption attempt).
B: too late — attending created the appearance of impropriety.
C: better, but the "adjusted proposal" hint should be reported.
D: upholds all four PMI values.

Step 4 — Application:
PMI Code: Fairness (proactively disclose real or potential conflicts, do not engage in activities creating conflicts). Honesty (do not engage in behaviour designed to personally gain). Responsibility (report unethical conduct).

Step 5 — Action:
Decline the dinner politely ("I appreciate the invitation but must decline during the evaluation period"). Document the dinner invitation and the "adjusted proposal" comment in writing. Report to your manager and procurement officer. The vendor may need to be disqualified if the conduct constitutes an improper offer.

Exam application: Any time you see "gift during procurement" or "vendor offers something extra" — the answer involves declining, disclosing, and likely reporting.

Q A senior developer publicly ridicules a junior developer's question in a team meeting. The junior goes quiet and does not ask questions again. Which psychological safety level has been damaged, and what should the PM do?
✅ Answer: B
Ridiculing a question attacks LEARNER safety — the belief that one can ask questions and make mistakes without humiliation. The PM must address the senior developer's behaviour (privately — modelling respect) and publicly re-establish that questions are welcome (e.g., asking questions themselves, thanking people who ask). Leaving it unaddressed signals the behaviour is acceptable and safety erodes for the whole team.
Q A fire alarm sounds during a heated design debate. Two team members insist on finishing the argument before evacuating. Which TKI conflict mode should the PM use, and why?
✅ Answer: C
Safety emergency = COMPETING (forcing) is correct. The PM directs immediate evacuation with no negotiation. Collaborating is PMI's general preference, but the exam tests situational judgment — in emergencies, unilateral direction is appropriate. This is why TKI has five modes: each fits a context.
Q According to research on project conflict, which is the MOST common source of conflict on projects?
✅ Answer: C
Schedules are the #1 source of project conflict, followed by priorities and resources. Personality clashes are the LEAST common — despite being the most talked about. This matters for diagnosis: when a "personality conflict" appears, dig for the underlying schedule or priority dispute driving it.
Q Using non-violent communication, which feedback statement is correctly structured?
✅ Answer: B
NVC structure: Observation (report arrived two days late) → Feeling (worried) → Need (committee needed it) → Request (flag delays early). It is specific, non-judgmental, and actionable. "Always late" and "unprofessional" are judgments; "do better" is vague; comparing to others shames. NVC addresses behaviour without attacking the person — preserving psychological safety.
Q A PM in a weak-matrix organisation needs a functional manager to release a key resource for two weeks. The functional manager has refused twice. Which influence approach is MOST likely to succeed?
✅ Answer: B
Influencing without authority: understand what the functional manager needs (their goals, constraints) and frame the request accordingly — perhaps the project outcome helps their department, or you can offer something in return (reciprocity). Escalation may work but damages the relationship and is a last resort. A memo citing authority is Legitimate power — weak in a matrix. Unofficial work is unethical.
Q A team retrospective is consistently silent — the same two people speak and everyone else says "no issues." What is the MOST likely cause and best response?
✅ Answer: B
Silence in retrospectives is a classic psychological safety symptom — people do not believe it is safe to raise problems. Format changes alone will not fix this. The PM should: (1) use anonymous input (sticky notes, digital tools) to lower the risk of speaking; (2) model vulnerability by sharing their own mistakes first; (3) respond visibly well when someone does raise an issue. Mandating participation increases fear.
Q Two team members disagree on database design. Both approaches would work. The decision is needed by tomorrow. The PM has moderate technical knowledge. Which TKI mode fits BEST?
✅ Answer: B
Both approaches are viable, both parties are competent, and there is (just) enough time — this is a COLLABORATING situation. A facilitated session (timeboxed to fit the deadline) to identify decision criteria and possibly merge approaches produces a better outcome AND preserves both relationships. Competing risks a suboptimal choice by a less-expert PM. Accommodating on seniority alone ignores merit.

📖 Worked Example: TKI in a Real Sprint

Situation: It is Sprint 4, Day 7. Two senior developers — Ankur (backend) and Neha (frontend) — have been in a 90-minute Zoom call arguing about whether the API should return paginated results or all records at once. The rest of the team is waiting. The sprint ends in 3 days.

Option 1 — Avoid (Wrong):
Scrum Master says "OK let's let you both think about it and discuss tomorrow." → Day 8: still no decision. Day 9: sprint fails. This is Withdrawing/Avoiding — the worst choice here.

Option 2 — Compete/Force (Situationally justified):
With 3 days left and a clear technical answer available, the Scrum Master or tech lead makes the call: "We use paginated results. Here is why: mobile network constraints, memory management on client side. Document your concerns in a retrospective item." This is competing — appropriate here because time is critical and the decision can be documented for future review.

Option 3 — Collaborate (Best if time permits):
If it is Day 3 of the sprint: "Let's spend 30 minutes agreeing on the decision criteria — what are we optimising for: load time, mobile performance, or server cost? Then we let the criteria decide." This is collaborating — produces the best outcome and builds team consensus for future decisions.

Option 4 — Compromise:
"Paginated for mobile; full results for desktop API?" — each side gives something. Not ideal technically, but resolves the conflict.

The lesson: PMI prefers Collaborate. But the exam tests SITUATIONAL judgment. In a time emergency → Compete. When both parties are equal and time allows → Collaborate. When relationship matters more than outcome → Compromise or Accommodate.

📖 Worked Example: Building Psychological Safety — Before and After

BEFORE (Low Psychological Safety team):
Sprint 7 review. PM asks "Any concerns about the payment gateway integration timeline?"
[Silence. Dev 2 knows there is a serious dependency issue but says nothing because the last time he raised a concern, the PM said "that is not your problem to solve — just write the code."]
Day 11 of Sprint 8: Dev 2 finally cannot hide it. The gateway has a 6-week vendor certification process that nobody planned for. Sprint fails. Sponsor furious. PM wonders "why did nobody raise this earlier?"

AFTER (PM rebuilds Psychological Safety over 3 sprints):

PM actions taken:
1. In Sprint Planning, PM opens: "Last sprint I missed some signals. If anyone has concerns this sprint — even half-formed ones — I want to hear them. There are no dumb concerns."
2. In Daily Scrum, PM models vulnerability: "I realised I hadn't mapped the vendor certification timeline. My oversight. Let us fix it together."
3. When Dev 3 tentatively raises a concern about the auth token expiry, PM says: "That is exactly the kind of thing I need to hear. Thank you, Priya. Let's investigate."
4. In retrospective, PM asks: "What would make it easier for people to raise concerns earlier?"

Sprint 10 result:
Dev 2 raises the gateway certification issue in Sprint Planning — 3 weeks before it would have been a crisis. Team adjusts the plan. Sprint succeeds. PM thanks Dev 2 publicly.

The principle: Psychological safety is not built by saying "please feel safe." It is built by demonstrating — repeatedly — that speaking up leads to a better outcome than silence.

📖 Worked Example: Non-Violent Communication (NVC) in Feedback

Situation: A team member, Rohan, submitted code without running tests. The code went to production and caused a 20-minute outage. This is the second time this month.

Traditional (blame-based) approach:
"Rohan, how could you do this again? You know the rules. This is completely unacceptable. You need to take this seriously."
→ Result: Rohan is defensive, embarrassed, disengaged. Does not understand what to change. Psychological safety damaged for the whole team.

NVC approach:
Observation: "Rohan, in the last two deployments, tests were not run before pushing to production."
Feeling: "I am concerned, because production incidents affect our customers and our team's credibility."
Need: "I need every deployment to go through the test gate — it is there to protect the product and the team."
Request: "Can you walk me through what happened both times? I want to understand if there is a process issue, a tool issue, or something I can help you with."

Result: Rohan explains that the CI/CD pipeline on his branch was broken and he did not know how to fix it. He was afraid to ask because he thought it would reflect badly. The PM helps fix the pipeline and adds a "broken pipeline" alert. Problem solved at root cause — not just symptoms.

The lesson: NVC (Observation → Feeling → Need → Request) addresses the BEHAVIOUR without attacking the PERSON. It creates the conditions for honest conversation. It is not weakness — it produces better outcomes.

Q Your sponsor instructs you to report the project status as "on track" to the steering committee, though you know the schedule has slipped by 3 weeks. She says the delay will be recovered before it matters. What should you do?
✅ Answer: B
Honesty (mandatory standard): do not engage in deceptive behaviour, half-truths, or withholding information. The status IS 3 weeks behind — that is a fact the steering committee needs for decision-making. The professional approach: report accurately AND present the recovery plan so the committee sees both the problem and the proposed solution. Private back-channel disclosure is still deception. Resigning avoids the responsibility to act ethically.
Q You are leading vendor selection. You realise one bidder is a company where your spouse works as a senior manager. Your spouse is not involved in the bid. What should you do?
✅ Answer: B
This is a clear conflict of interest — a close personal relationship with a bidder. Fairness (mandatory): proactively and fully disclose any real or POTENTIAL conflict, and refrain from participating until stakeholders consent. Whether your spouse is directly involved is irrelevant — the PERCEIVED conflict undermines confidence in the process. Disclose in writing and recuse.
Q In a country where your project operates, it is customary to pay a "processing fee" to a government clerk to expedite permits. Without it, permits take 6 months instead of 2 weeks. Your schedule depends on the 2-week timeline. What should you do?
✅ Answer: B
A facilitation payment to a government official to expedite a service is a bribe — prohibited by the PMI Code (Fairness, Honesty) and illegal under laws like the US FCPA and UK Bribery Act regardless of local custom. Using a contractor as an intermediary is still bribery. The correct path: refuse, assess the schedule impact, escalate, and explore legitimate options (expedited legal processes, schedule re-baseline). Ethics takes priority over schedule.
Q Using PMI's Ethical Decision-Making Framework, what is the FIRST step when facing an ethical dilemma?
✅ Answer: C
The EDMF starts with ASSESSMENT — ensuring you have all the facts. Just like A.R.T. for project decisions, ethical decisions begin with understanding the situation fully: What actually happened? Is it legal? Who is affected? Does it violate the Code? Only after assessment do you consider alternatives, analyse, apply principles, and act.
Q A junior team member tells you in confidence that a senior team member has been sexually harassing her. She asks you not to do anything because she fears retaliation. What should you do?
✅ Answer: C
Respect (mandatory): do not act in an abusive manner; Responsibility (mandatory): report unethical conduct. Harassment is a mandatory-standard violation that MUST be reported — you cannot honour a request to conceal it. The PM should explain this to her compassionately, report through proper channels, and ensure protection from retaliation (also a mandatory standard). Quietly moving her punishes the victim and lets the behaviour continue.
Q You used an AI tool to generate a risk assessment for your project. The output looks thorough. What is your ethical obligation before presenting it to stakeholders?
✅ Answer: B
Honesty requires transparency about AI use; Responsibility requires you to remain accountable for the content. The PM must validate AI outputs (AI can hallucinate, encode bias, or miss context) and disclose AI assistance. Presenting it as purely your own work is deceptive. Banning AI use entirely is unnecessary — the 2026 ECO explicitly expects PMs to use AI responsibly.

📖 Worked Example: Applying All Four PMI Values in One Decision

Situation: You are PM for a ₹15 crore ERP implementation at a manufacturing company. Week 8: your vendor's on-site consultant privately tells you that a junior team member from the client side has been sharing confidential implementation plans with a competitor who is bidding for Phase 2. You cannot prove it — it is the consultant's word.

Applying each PMI value:

Responsibility:
You cannot ignore this. Responsibility (mandatory): report unethical or illegal conduct to appropriate management. BUT — you must act on facts, not rumour. Responsibility also means "only bring complaints based on facts." Do not accuse without evidence.
→ Action: Document what the consultant told you verbatim, with date and time.

Respect:
The accused team member is innocent until there is evidence. You cannot confront them, exclude them, or treat them as guilty.
→ Action: Do not change the team member's access or responsibilities yet. Do not discuss with others.

Fairness:
You have an obligation to the client organisation — they are trusting you with their confidential implementation data. You also cannot let an unsubstantiated accusation destroy a junior employee's career.
→ Action: Report to the client's senior sponsor and suggest an internal investigation process — not your own investigation.

Honesty:
You must be truthful about what you know and what you do not know. Do not overstate or understate the situation.
→ Action: "A vendor team member has reported to me a concern about potential information leakage. I cannot verify it. I am bringing it to your attention because it involves your organisational security. I recommend you engage HR and security to investigate."

Result: You act with all four values simultaneously. You report (Responsibility). You do not accuse (Respect + Fairness). You are transparent about the uncertainty (Honesty). The investigation is not yours to conduct — it is the client's.

📖 Worked Example: Conflict of Interest — Three Levels

Level 1 — Clear and Obvious:
Your brother's company is bidding for a vendor contract on your project. This is a clear conflict of interest.
→ Disclose in writing immediately. Recuse yourself from the evaluation. Let someone else lead the vendor selection. Do not even review the proposals.

Level 2 — Subtle but Real:
You interviewed for a job at Vendor B three months ago. They are now shortlisted for a ₹2 crore contract. You did not get the job offer.
→ Still a conflict. Disclose to your manager: "I had discussions about employment with Vendor B in [month]. I want to ensure there is no perceived bias in my evaluation." Ask your manager if you should recuse or if full disclosure is sufficient. Let them decide.

Level 3 — Perceived but Not Actual:
Your spouse works in the accounts department of a company that is in the same industry as a bidding vendor, but has no connection to the bid.
→ Probably no conflict, but consider disclosing anyway. The test: would a reasonable observer think your judgment might be influenced? If yes → disclose. If no reasonable person would think so → use judgment. When in doubt, disclose.

The PMI rule: Fairness (mandatory standard): "Proactively and FULLY disclose any real or POTENTIAL conflicts of interest." The word "potential" is key — you do not wait until there is an actual problem. Disclose early, transparently, in writing.

Exam trap: "The PM's friend is a senior employee at the bidding vendor. The PM evaluates fairly and the friend's company wins on merit. Is this acceptable?"
→ NO. Even if the decision was fair, the PROCESS was compromised by the undisclosed relationship. The mandatory standard requires disclosure regardless of the actual outcome.

EEF vs. OPA — Critical Distinction

Enterprise Environmental Factors (EEF)

Conditions NOT under the project team's control that influence, constrain, or direct the project. Can be INTERNAL or EXTERNAL.

  • Organisational culture and structure
  • Government or industry regulations and standards
  • Market conditions and competitive landscape
  • Infrastructure: physical environment, tools, IT systems
  • Commercial databases (published benchmarks, pricing)
  • Geographic distribution of facilities and resources

Organisational Process Assets (OPA)

Plans, processes, policies, procedures, and knowledge bases specific to the organisation. The project team can USE and UPDATE these.

  • Templates, forms, and standardised processes
  • Lessons learned repository from past projects
  • Historical project information and performance data
  • Change control procedures and CCB charter
  • Financial controls and reporting requirements
  • Project management information systems (PMIS)
✅
EEF = the environment you work IN (external constraints you cannot control). OPA = the organisational KNOWLEDGE you can work WITH (internal assets you can use and update). Both are inputs to most planning processes.

Benefits Management Plan — Contents

  • Target benefits: Tangible (revenue increase, cost savings) AND intangible (brand reputation, employee satisfaction)
  • Strategic alignment: How the benefits connect to organisational strategy and objectives
  • Timeframe: When benefits will be realised — short-term, long-term, ongoing, or by phase
  • Benefits owner: Who is accountable for monitoring, recording, and reporting benefits realisation
  • Metrics: Direct and indirect measures used to confirm benefits are being realised
  • Assumptions and risks: Factors expected to be in place for benefits to occur, and risks to realisation

Governance and Project Governance

  • Organisational governance: Framework, functions, processes that guide strategy and operations at the enterprise level
  • Project governance: Framework for decisions, oversight, and controls specific to the project — aligned to organisational governance
  • Project governance includes: decision-making authority, change control, reporting requirements, acceptance criteria, stage gates
  • PMBOK 8: Governance is a full performance domain — it integrates risk, quality, change, and knowledge management
Q A PM is assigned to a project in a functional organisation. They need additional resources from the engineering department. Who must they negotiate with?
✅ Answer: C
In a functional organisation, the Functional Manager controls all resources within their department. PMs in functional organisations have little or no authority over resources outside their direct team. Any resource requests must be negotiated with and approved by the Functional Manager — this is one of the key challenges (and exam traps) of working in functional or weak-matrix structures.
Q A project manager discovers the company has existing templates and a lessons learned repository from similar past projects. What type of asset is this?
✅ Answer: B
Templates and lessons learned repositories are Organisational Process Assets (OPA) — they are organisational knowledge specific to the company that the project team can use and update. EEF are conditions the team cannot control (regulations, market conditions, culture). OPA are assets the team actively leverages to improve project performance.
Q Which document defines the processes for creating, tracking, and sustaining project benefits AFTER the project closes?
✅ Answer: D
The Benefits Management Plan documents how and when benefits will be delivered, the mechanisms to measure them, who is accountable for benefits realisation, and how benefits will be sustained after project closure. It differs from the Business Case (which justifies the project investment) and the Project Charter (which authorises it). Practice Questions — Business Environment (2026 ECO)

Q A new environmental regulation is announced that could significantly impact your project's manufacturing approach. What should the PM do FIRST?
✅ Answer: C
A.R.T.: First ASSESS — understand the regulation and its specific impact on scope, schedule, cost, and quality. Then update the Risk Register with any identified risks or opportunities. Only after thorough assessment should you proceed to review the plan and take action (raise change request if needed). Halting all work is extreme without analysis. Seeking exemption is unlikely and premature.
Q Your organisation is adopting an AI tool to assist with project risk analysis. As PM, what is your PRIMARY responsibility regarding this tool?
✅ Answer: B
PMI's position on AI: it augments the PM but never replaces PM judgment. The PM must evaluate AI outputs critically, validate them with expert judgment, document AI usage, and ensure use is ethical, responsible, and aligned with organisational AI policy. Delegating ALL risk identification to AI abdicates PM responsibility. Rejecting AI entirely ignores its value. There is no restriction to only quantitative analysis.
Q Midway through project execution, stakeholder analysis reveals that a key department is highly resistant to the organisational change the project will deliver. What should the PM do?
✅ Answer: C
Organisational Change Management (a 2026 ECO Business Environment task): resistance must be understood and addressed proactively. Assess root cause first (workload concerns? Fear of job loss? Unclear benefits?), engage the department in dialogue, and update the change management approach to address their specific concerns. Escalating immediately bypasses the PM's responsibility to manage stakeholders. Removing them or ignoring resistance will create bigger problems at implementation.
Q A project has been running for 6 months. The original business case assumed a 20% revenue increase, but market conditions have shifted significantly. The expected benefit is now only 8%. What should the PM do?
✅ Answer: C
Benefits realisation is a continuous responsibility (2026 ECO Business Environment). When circumstances change the projected value significantly, the PM must reassess the business case and benefits management plan, quantify the revised benefit, and present findings to the sponsor — who has authority to make the go/no-go decision. Sunk cost fallacy (option A) is not a valid reason to continue a project that no longer justifies investment. Simply updating the risk register (option D) without escalating the business case change is insufficient.

Q A stakeholder is aware of an upcoming system change and understands how to use the new system, but actively resists adopting it. Using ADKAR, where is the barrier?
✅ Answer: B
ADKAR: Awareness (they know) ✓. Knowledge (they understand how) ✓. But they RESIST — the barrier is DESIRE. More training (Knowledge) will not help — they already know how. The PM must address WHY they resist: what do they fear losing? What is in it for them? Desire barriers require engagement, addressing concerns, and demonstrating personal benefit — not more information or training.
Q A senior engineer with 20 years of experience is retiring. Their knowledge of the legacy system is undocumented and critical. What type of knowledge is this, and how should it be transferred?
✅ Answer: B
Experience-based knowledge that is hard to articulate is TACIT knowledge. It transfers through relationship-based methods: shadowing, pairing, mentoring, communities of practice, and storytelling — not through documentation alone. While some can be documented (making it explicit), the judgment and intuition require hands-on transfer. Planning this transfer BEFORE departure is a Business Environment (knowledge management) responsibility.
Q A new data protection regulation comes into effect in 6 months affecting your project. The compliance requirement will add $50K to the budget. What should the PM do?
✅ Answer: C
Legal/regulatory compliance is MANDATORY — there is no option to ignore, defer, or seek exemption. The regulation WILL apply, so it is a certainty (issue/constraint), not a risk. The PM must: assess impact → raise a change request for the additional scope and $50K → update the compliance management plan → ensure the project achieves compliance by the deadline. Six months is enough time IF action starts now.
Q Which document should a PM reference to verify that a project is still aligned with organisational strategy?
✅ Answer: B
The Business Case documents WHY the project exists — the business need, strategic alignment, expected benefits, and financial justification. It is the reference for whether the project still makes sense strategically. If the business case becomes invalid (market changes, strategy shifts), the project should be re-evaluated. The Charter authorises the project; the PM Plan describes how; neither validates ongoing strategic alignment.
Q In Kotter's 8-Step Change Model, why are "short-term wins" important?
✅ Answer: B
Kotter's Step 6 — Generate Short-Term Wins — creates visible evidence that the change is working. This builds credibility for the change initiative, converts skeptics, rewards early adopters, and maintains momentum. Without short-term wins, people lose faith and resistance grows. Short-term wins complement (not replace) the vision from Step 3.

📖 Worked Example: EEF vs OPA — Spotting the Difference

Project: Launching a co-lending platform for a non-banking financial company (NBFC)

ENTERPRISE ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS (things you CANNOT control):
• RBI's new co-lending guidelines (Master Direction 2023) — you must comply, cannot change them
• CIBIL and Experian credit bureau APIs — external systems you depend on
• Interest rate environment — affects product viability but PM cannot control
• Fintech startup culture — fast-moving, tolerates ambiguity
• COVID-era WFH norms still affecting your team's in-person availability
→ These CONSTRAIN and INFLUENCE your project. You ADAPT to them.

ORGANISATIONAL PROCESS ASSETS (things you CAN use and update):
• Your company's project charter template (you fill it in; you can suggest updates to the template)
• Lessons learned from the 2022 mobile lending app project
• Standard risk register format from the PMO
• Internal coding standards and security review checklist
• Historical velocity data from previous agile projects
→ These are TOOLS and KNOWLEDGE you leverage. You can also UPDATE them (lessons learned → future projects).

Exam tip — the quick test:
"Can the project team change this?" → YES = OPA. NO = EEF.
"Is this internal to the organisation?" → Usually YES = OPA. External market/legal/physical = EEF.

📖 Worked Example: ADKAR — Diagnosing the Barrier

Project: Implementing a new digital underwriting system at an insurance company. 45 underwriters will move from email-based approvals to a workflow tool.

The problem: 3 months after go-live, only 30% of underwriters are using the new system. The rest still use email and spreadsheets.

ADKAR Diagnosis (interview 10 underwriters):

Awareness — Do they know about the change?
"Yes, we heard about it." → A ✅

Desire — Do they want to change?
"Honestly? No. Our spreadsheets work fine. The new system seems designed for IT people, not underwriters." → D ❌

Knowledge — Do they know HOW to use it?
(Irrelevant — they don't want to use it, so they haven't bothered learning)

Root cause identified: DESIRE barrier.
The training (Knowledge) and the mandate (Awareness) did nothing because nobody addressed WHY an underwriter would WANT to change.

Correct intervention:
• Involve 3 senior underwriters in redesigning the workflow screens for their specific use case
• Demonstrate "what's in it for me": the new system shows your SLA compliance score automatically — good for performance reviews
• Give early adopters a "Digital Underwriter" recognition badge in internal comms
• Walk the floor with the system for 2 weeks — hands-on support

Three months later: 78% adoption. The remaining 22% are the near-retirement underwriters who have been given a 6-month transition period.

Lesson: Never jump to Knowledge/training when the barrier is Desire. They are two different problems requiring two different interventions.

Q A project delivered a new CRM system on time, on budget, with all features. Six months later, only 20% of sales staff use it; the rest still use spreadsheets. According to PMBOK 8 value principles, how should this be assessed?
✅ Answer: B
PMBOK 8 and the 2026 ECO define success by VALUE delivered, not outputs produced. A CRM with 20% adoption has not achieved its outcome (changed sales behaviour) or benefits (pipeline visibility, efficiency). The output was delivered; the value was not. This is why adoption rate is a critical outcome metric and why change management is now a Business Environment task.
Q A value stream map shows a feature takes 60 days from request to production, with 9 days of actual development and testing work. What is the Process Cycle Efficiency, and what does it indicate?
✅ Answer: A
PCE = VA Time ÷ Lead Time = 9 ÷ 60 = 15%. This means 85% of the lead time (51 days) is non-value-added — waiting for approvals, queues, handoffs. This is typical for unoptimised processes. The improvement opportunity is NOT to make developers faster (they only account for 9 days) but to eliminate the 51 days of waste — the classic Lean insight.
Q Which of the following is a correctly written Key Result?
✅ Answer: C
A Key Result must be MEASURABLE (a number), OUTCOME-focused (not an activity), and TIME-BOUND. "Increase retention from 22% to 40% by Q3" has a baseline, target, and deadline, and measures an outcome. "Launch the app" and "complete sprints" are activities/outputs, not results. "Improve satisfaction" has no metric.
Q After project closure, who is accountable for measuring and reporting whether the projected $2M annual savings were realised?
✅ Answer: B
Benefits typically materialise AFTER the project closes — often over years. The PM and team have moved on. Accountability sits with the BENEFITS OWNER — a business-side role (usually in operations) named in the Benefits Management Plan. The PM's responsibility is to ensure tracking mechanisms and ownership are in place at handover. The PMO may aggregate and audit but does not own individual benefits.
Q Two features are competing for the next sprint. Feature A: Cost of Delay $40K/week, size 8 points. Feature B: Cost of Delay $30K/week, size 3 points. Using WSJF, which goes first?
✅ Answer: B
WSJF = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Size. Feature A: 40 ÷ 8 = 5. Feature B: 30 ÷ 3 = 10. Feature B has the higher WSJF and goes first. The logic: B delivers most of its value quickly (small job), then A can start. If you did A first, you would forgo $30K/week on B for 8 points of effort. WSJF favours small, valuable jobs.
Q A PM wants to know during the project whether the new system will achieve its target benefit of 50% faster processing. Which is the BEST leading indicator to track?
✅ Answer: B
A LEADING indicator predicts future value while there is still time to act. Pilot-group processing time on a beta release directly measures whether the 50% target is achievable — during the project. Annual savings, post-launch surveys, and year-end ROI are LAGGING indicators — they confirm value only after it is too late to change course.

Thursday — Schedule Risk Analysis:
AI runs Monte Carlo simulation on the schedule (10,000 iterations) in 30 seconds — a task that would take a human with Crystal Ball 2 hours.

→ What the PM does NOT do: commit to the 87% date as a firm deadline without buffer.

• Making the final go/no-go decision on the risk response (PM is accountable)
• Handling the emotionally charged conversation with the sponsor about the delay
• Any analysis requiring confidential client data fed into an unapproved external tool

Q An AI scheduling tool forecasts project completion on 20 October with 85% confidence. The team's bottom-up estimate says 5 December. What should the PM do?
✅ Answer: C
A large divergence between AI forecast and team estimate is a signal to INVESTIGATE, not to pick a side or average. Perhaps the AI lacks context on a new technology; perhaps the team is padding estimates. Understanding WHY they differ produces a better estimate than either alone. PMI's model: AI augments judgment — it does not replace it, nor is it ignored.
Q A PM uses a public AI chatbot to draft a stakeholder communication, pasting in the full stakeholder register including personal contact details and engagement assessments. What is the primary concern?
✅ Answer: B
Pasting personal data and confidential assessments into a public AI tool is a data protection and confidentiality breach — violating the Respect value (property rights), likely data protection law (GDPR etc.), and organisational policy. This is the #1 practical AI risk on projects. The PM should have anonymised the data or used an approved internal tool.
Q An AI resource-allocation tool consistently recommends the same three senior engineers for high-visibility work, while junior and female engineers are recommended for maintenance tasks. What should the PM do?
✅ Answer: B
The pattern suggests algorithmic bias — the AI is likely reproducing historical patterns (senior engineers got past opportunities, so it predicts they will succeed again). Fairness (PMI Code) requires equal access to opportunity. The PM should investigate, apply human judgment to allocation, and ensure development opportunities are distributed fairly. Blind compliance perpetuates bias; forced equal distribution ignores genuine capability differences.
Q A regulator audits a pharmaceutical project and asks which risk assessments were AI-generated. The PM did not track this. What ethical and governance failure does this represent?
✅ Answer: B
Transparency (Honesty) and accountability (Responsibility) require that AI-assisted artifacts be identified and traceable — especially in regulated industries where audit trails are mandatory. The AI governance framework should specify how AI use is disclosed and documented. Without this, the PM cannot demonstrate that AI outputs were properly validated.
Q Which activity is LEAST appropriate to delegate to an AI tool?
✅ Answer: C
Conflict mediation requires emotional intelligence, trust, relationship, and nuanced human judgment — core People-domain skills where AI adds no value and may cause harm. Drafting reports, running simulations, and extracting action items are pattern-based tasks where AI excels (with human review). PMI's guidance: use AI for analysis and drafting; keep human relationships human.
Q The organisation has no AI usage policy. A team member proposes using an AI tool to generate test cases. What should the PM do?
✅ Answer: C
Absence of policy is not permission or prohibition — it is a governance gap the PM should address. The Business Environment task "Manage and implement AI" expects the PM to assess risks, establish appropriate guardrails for the project, and escalate the need for organisational policy. Blanket prohibition forgoes value; blanket permission ignores risk; waiting indefinitely stalls the project.

What Is Tailoring?

Tailoring is the deliberate adaptation of the project management approach, governance, and processes to make them more suitable for the specific project environment. It is driven by the 6 principles and guided by organisational values and culture.

⚠️
Tailoring is NOT "do whatever you want." It requires justification, alignment with principles, and continuous assessment. The goal is to choose the MOST APPROPRIATE practices for the context — not the most convenient.

1. Select ApproachChoose predictive, adaptive, or hybrid as the starting pointDeliverable uncertainty, team maturity, regulatory requirements, customer expectations, organisational culture

3. Tailor for the ProjectFurther refine based on project-specific characteristicsProject size, complexity, risk level, stakeholder needs, delivery cadence, team location
4. Implement Ongoing ImprovementContinuously assess and adjust throughout the project lifecycleRetrospectives, lessons learned, quality metrics, stakeholder feedback, issue patterns

Q A project manager is about to start a project developing a new medical device. Requirements are clearly defined by regulatory authorities. What development approach is most appropriate?
✅ Answer: B
When requirements are clearly defined (by regulatory standards in this case), change is costly/regulated, and compliance is critical — a Predictive (waterfall) approach is most appropriate. The detailed schedule, formal change control, and documented baselines of predictive PM align with regulatory requirements. Agile requires flexibility in scope; regulatory environments often prohibit that.
Q A project team in an adaptive lifecycle is struggling with scope management — stakeholders keep requesting changes mid-sprint that disrupt the team. What is the BEST approach?
✅ Answer: C
In agile/Scrum, changes are embraced — but they go into the PRODUCT BACKLOG, not directly into the current sprint. The Product Owner manages and prioritises the backlog. New requests during a sprint should be captured in the backlog and prioritised for future sprints. Sprint Goals must be protected. Educating stakeholders on the process (add to backlog → prioritise → next sprint) is the correct agile response.

Q A PM is starting a 6-person, 3-month internal tool development project in a fast-moving startup. Which tailoring decision is MOST appropriate?
✅ Answer: B
Small team, short duration, low criticality, innovative culture → lightweight tailoring. A one-page charter, Kanban for flow, weekly demos for stakeholder feedback, and a simple risk board provide adequate governance without overhead. "No process" is never correct — even startups need some governance. Full PMBOK or phase gates would be over-tailoring for this context.
Q A team adopts Scrum but decides to skip retrospectives "because we don't have time." Six months later, the same problems keep recurring. What tailoring mistake was made?
✅ Answer: B
Retrospectives are Scrum's continuous improvement mechanism. Removing them without understanding their purpose is cherry-picking — taking the convenient parts of a method while dropping the parts that make it work. The recurring problems are the direct consequence: no mechanism to inspect and adapt the process. Tailoring must be justified by project characteristics, not by convenience.
Q A medical device project has both regulated hardware (FDA-controlled) and companion mobile app software. How should governance be tailored?
✅ Answer: C
Hybrid tailoring applies the right governance to each component. Hardware under FDA regulation needs formal gates, traceability, and documentation. Software can use sprints with regulatory documentation built into the Definition of Done. A unified risk register and integrated schedule ensure the components stay aligned. Uniform heavy governance slows software unnecessarily; uniform light governance fails regulatory compliance; separate projects lose integration.
Q A PM documents that the project will skip quantitative risk analysis. A PMO auditor asks why. Which response demonstrates correct tailoring practice?
✅ Answer: B
Correct tailoring is JUSTIFIED by project characteristics (size, complexity, risk threshold) and DOCUMENTED in the PM plan. Option B cites specific criteria and confirms documentation. "No time" and "don't know how" are convenience/capability excuses, not tailoring rationale. "Optional in PMBOK" is true but not a justification — the question is whether it is appropriate for THIS project.
Q Midway through a predictive project, requirements are changing every week and rework is mounting. What does this diagnostic signal suggest?
✅ Answer: B
Requirements churn and rework in a predictive project signal that the approach does not fit the actual uncertainty level. The tailoring diagnostic response is to re-tailor: shift toward adaptive or hybrid for the volatile components, add prototyping, shorten feedback loops. Tightening change control fights the symptom, not the cause. Extending the schedule accepts the waste. Replacing people is never a first response.

📖 Worked Example: Tailoring Decision — Small Project

Context: A 3-person team at a startup needs to build an internal expense-tracking tool. Budget: ₹3 lakh. Duration: 6 weeks. Team knows the requirements well. No regulatory constraints. Sponsor is the CTO who sits 5 metres away.

Tailoring decisions:

Life Cycle: Adaptive (Kanban). No sprints needed — work flows continuously. Daily standup: 10 minutes (informal).

Charter: One-page project canvas — purpose, scope boundary, success criteria, budget. Signed in 30 minutes. NOT a 20-page document.

Change Control: PO (the CTO) adds/removes backlog items as needed. No CCB. Any change affecting the 6-week deadline or ₹3L budget discussed face-to-face.

Risk: A simple risk board (4 stickies) on the Kanban board. No formal risk register needed at this scale.

Quality: Definition of Done: code reviewed by one peer, deployed to a test environment, tested by the user who requested the feature. No formal QA plan.

Status Reporting: The Kanban board IS the status report — the CTO walks past it daily. Weekly 15-minute sync. No written status reports.

Lessons Learned: 1-hour retro at project close. 1-page summary added to the company wiki.

What was removed: Formal CCB, stakeholder register, communications management plan, risk register, quality management plan, formal status reports, phase gates.
Justification: Small team, co-located, well-understood scope, no regulatory constraints, sponsor directly accessible. All removed elements would create overhead without value.

📖 Worked Example: Tailoring Decision — Large Regulated Project

Context: A banking group is implementing a new SWIFT payment system across 12 countries. Budget: ₹180 crore. Duration: 36 months. RBI, SEBI, and 12 national regulators involved. 45 vendors. 200-person team across India, Singapore, UK.

Tailoring decisions:

Life Cycle: Predictive (Waterfall) for infrastructure and regulatory components. Agile (Scrum, 2-week sprints) for configuration and testing components. Hybrid.

Charter: Full 20-page document with legal sign-off, authority matrix, budget approval chain, regulatory references.

Change Control: Full CCB with 8 members (Sponsor, PMO Head, Regulatory Liaison, CTO, Finance, 2 external members). Written change requests mandatory for all changes. 5-day turnaround for standard changes; emergency path for regulatory-driven changes.

Risk: Full risk register (120+ risks identified). Quantitative risk analysis (Monte Carlo) for schedule and cost. Risk review every 2 weeks with steering committee.

Quality: Full quality management plan. ISO 20022 compliance verification. External audit at Phase 2 gate. Regulatory acceptance criteria formally documented.

Status Reporting: Weekly written status to PMO. Monthly steering committee dashboard. Quarterly regulatory report. Country-specific reports for each of 12 regulators.

Tailoring justification: 36 months, ₹180 crore, 12 regulators, 45 vendors, 200 people. Full governance is justified — removing ANY of these elements creates regulatory, financial, or operational risk at scale.

The lesson: The same PM framework applied to both projects — but the CALIBRATION is completely different. Tailoring is not about cutting corners. It is about right-sizing governance to the risk and complexity of the specific project.

📖 Worked Example: Tailoring Diagnostic — When to Re-Tailor

Project: A digital KYC platform (hybrid approach, 9 months in, 3 months to go).

Symptoms observed in the last 3 sprints:
• Sprint reviews consistently show 60% completion (never near the goal)
• The same blockers appear in every retrospective and are never resolved
• Business stakeholders stopped attending sprint reviews ("nothing useful ever ships")
• The team spends 30% of sprint time in governance meetings

Diagnostic questions (PMBOK 8):
1. Are processes adding overhead without value? → Yes: 30% in governance meetings is excessive
2. Are sprint goals realistic? → No: consistently 60% hit rate means poor estimation or scope instability
3. Are impediments being removed? → No: same blockers appearing for 3 sprints
4. Is the development approach right? → Partially: hybrid works, but the predictive governance overhead is too heavy

Re-tailoring decisions:
• Reduce governance meetings from weekly to bi-weekly; move minor decisions to async Slack approvals
• Add a "blocker escalation" protocol: any blocker unresolved for >48 hours is automatically escalated to PM
• Sprint review: add a "mini-demo" of in-progress items (not just completed) to maintain stakeholder visibility
• Estimation: conduct a one-sprint calibration exercise — re-estimate using yesterday's weather method

Result: Sprint 10 completion rate: 80%. Governance meeting time: 12% of sprint. Stakeholders re-engaged. Two blockers resolved within 24 hours of the new protocol.

Scrum: 3-5-3 Rule

3 Roles

  • Product Owner: Owns and PRIORITISES the Product Backlog; maximises product value; single point of accountability; only ONE person (not a committee); accepts/rejects sprint output
  • Scrum Master: SERVANT LEADER; removes impediments; facilitates ceremonies; coaches team on Scrum; shields team from interruptions; helps PO with backlog refinement — NOT a traditional PM
  • Developers: Cross-functional; self-organising; 3–9 members; designs, builds, and tests the increment; creates their own Sprint Backlog

3 Artifacts

  • Product Backlog: Ordered list of all known work for the product; owned by PO; never complete; evolves throughout the product's life
  • Sprint Backlog: Selected Product Backlog items for this sprint + plan to deliver them; owned by Developers; visible to all
  • Increment: Sum of ALL completed backlog items to date; MUST meet Definition of Done; must be usable; potentially shippable after every sprint

Sprint Review vs. Sprint Retrospective — Know the Difference

Sprint Review

  • Focus: Improve the PRODUCT (what was built)
  • Attendees: Scrum Team + Stakeholders, customers, management
  • Output: Updated Product Backlog; next steps; stakeholder feedback
  • Order: Second-to-last event of the Sprint
  • Duration: ≤4 hours for a 4-week Sprint

Sprint Retrospective

  • Focus: Improve the PROCESS and TEAM (how it was built)
  • Attendees: Scrum Team ONLY — no external stakeholders
  • Output: Actionable improvements committed for next sprint
  • Order: LAST event of the Sprint (after Review, before next Planning)
  • Duration: ≤3 hours for a 4-week Sprint
🔑
Sprint Review = improve the PRODUCT. Sprint Retrospective = improve the PROCESS. Retro always comes AFTER Review and BEFORE the next Sprint Planning. This order is tested.

Scrum Rules — Non-Negotiable

  • Sprint duration is FIXED — never extended or shortened once started
  • Sprint Goal should NOT change during a Sprint — protects team focus
  • ONLY the Product Owner can cancel a Sprint, and ONLY if the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete
  • Scrum Master does NOT assign tasks, manage resources, or act as project manager
  • The TEAM creates their own estimates — PM/SM never estimates FOR them
  • Definition of Done (DoD): owned by Developers; defines "complete" for any increment; if DoD not met, item is NOT done
  • Backlog Refinement: ongoing; recommended ≤10% of Sprint capacity; not a formal Scrum event

Kanban Principles

  • Visualise the workflow — make all work visible on a Kanban board
  • WIP Limits — cap items in each stage; prevent overloading; force resolution of bottlenecks
  • Manage flow — focus on smooth, fast, predictable delivery
  • Make process policies explicit — everyone knows the rules of the workflow
  • Bottleneck: When work piles up in one column, stop pulling new work — focus the whole team on clearing the bottleneck. Work from the RIGHT-MOST full column first.
  • Continuous flow — unlike Scrum, there are no timeboxed sprints; work flows continuously

Hybrid Approaches — When and How

  • Project has BOTH stable components (use predictive) and volatile/uncertain components (use adaptive)
  • Organisation is TRANSITIONING to agile but not fully there yet — keep some predictive guardrails
  • Regulatory requirements mandate specific deliverables (predictive) while development is agile
  • Example: Predictive for hardware, infrastructure, and compliance documentation; agile for software development; Kanban for support and maintenance

Q During Sprint Planning, the team selects items from the Product Backlog and commits to a Sprint Goal. Who is responsible for creating the estimates for the selected backlog items?
✅ Answer: C
In Scrum, DEVELOPERS create their own estimates. Only the people doing the work can accurately estimate effort — they have the technical expertise (SME). The Product Owner provides priorities and business value; the Scrum Master facilitates. External estimation by management violates the self-organising principle and reduces estimate accuracy and team ownership.
Q A stakeholder requests a new high-priority feature halfway through a Sprint. What should happen?
✅ Answer: C
New requests go to the PRODUCT BACKLOG — not into the current sprint. The Sprint Goal and Sprint Backlog should not change during a Sprint (unless the Sprint Goal itself becomes obsolete). The Product Owner adds it to the backlog, prioritises it, and the team will consider it in the next Sprint Planning. Only the PO can cancel a Sprint, and only if the Sprint Goal becomes invalid.
Q A Scrum team's velocity over the last 5 sprints has been: 22, 18, 20, 24, 21 story points. The next sprint has 30 story points planned. What is the concern?
✅ Answer: B
Average velocity = (22+18+20+24+21)/5 = 105/5 = 21 story points per sprint. Planning 30 points is ~43% above the team's historical capacity — a significant overcommitment. Sustainable pace is a core agile principle. Overcommitting leads to burnout, incomplete work, quality issues, and eroded trust with stakeholders. The team should plan to approximately 21 points, not 30.
Q What is the PRIMARY difference between a Sprint Review and a Sprint Retrospective?
✅ Answer: B
Sprint Review = inspects and adapts the PRODUCT (what was built) — includes stakeholders. Sprint Retrospective = inspects and adapts the TEAM and PROCESS (how it was built) — Scrum Team only, no external stakeholders. The Review comes BEFORE the Retrospective at the end of a sprint. Both are mandatory Scrum events.

Q A large organisation with 80 developers across 10 teams wants to coordinate their agile work with quarterly planning cycles. Which scaling framework is MOST likely appropriate?
✅ Answer: B
SAFe is designed for large enterprises with 50–125+ people organised into Agile Release Trains, using quarterly Program Increment (PI) Planning. 80 developers across 10 teams with quarterly cycles fits SAFe precisely. Scrum of Scrums is lightweight coordination for a few teams. LeSS handles 2–8 teams on one product. Nexus handles 3–9 teams — closer but SAFe's quarterly PI Planning matches the stated requirement.
Q A software team builds several features that were never requested by users, based on what developers thought would be "cool." In Lean terms, this is which type of waste?
✅ Answer: C
Building things nobody asked for is OVERPRODUCTION — the classic Lean waste. It consumed effort, added complexity, and delivered no value. This is the same concept as "gold plating" in predictive PM. Overproduction is considered the worst waste because it causes other wastes (inventory of unused features, defects in unnecessary code, etc.).
Q On a Cumulative Flow Diagram, the "In Testing" band has been widening steadily for three weeks. What does this indicate?
✅ Answer: B
On a CFD, each band represents items in a workflow state. A WIDENING band means items are entering that state faster than they are leaving — work is accumulating there. This indicates a BOTTLENECK in testing. The team should investigate (test capacity? environment issues? unclear acceptance criteria?) and address it — possibly by having developers help with testing.
Q A pharmaceutical company uses agile for drug discovery software but must produce regulatory documentation for FDA submission. What is the correct approach?
✅ Answer: B
Agile CAN work in regulated environments — documentation is produced INCREMENTALLY as part of each sprint (added to Definition of Done), not skipped or deferred. This is a hybrid approach: agile development with regulatory documentation as a sprint deliverable. Retroactive documentation is risky and often incomplete. Switching to predictive at the end creates a "big bang" documentation phase — exactly what agile avoids.
Q Which statement about Disciplined Agile (DA) is correct?
✅ Answer: B
Disciplined Agile (DA) is PMI's agile approach — a TOOLKIT (not a prescriptive framework) that helps teams "choose their way of working" (WoW). It incorporates Scrum, Kanban, XP, SAFe, and other practices, letting teams select what fits their context. It is hybrid-friendly and scalable from small teams to enterprises. It does not replace other frameworks — it draws from them.

Scrum in Practice — A Full Sprint Walk-Through

Project: Building a UPI payments feature for a neobank app
Team: 1 Product Owner (Priya), 1 Scrum Master (Rahul), 5 Developers
Sprint: 2 weeks | Velocity: ~35 story points

DAY 0 — SPRINT PLANNING (≤4 hours for 2-week sprint):
Product Backlog top items (already refined):
• [8pts] As a user, I want to link my bank account via UPI ID
• [5pts] As a user, I want to initiate a payment with PIN confirmation
• [5pts] As a user, I want to see my last 10 transactions
• [3pts] As a user, I want to receive push notification on payment success
• [8pts] As a user, I want to set a daily transaction limit
• [5pts] Performance: payment API response <2 sec under 1000 concurrent users
Total: 34 points. Sprint Goal: "A user can link an account and complete a basic UPI payment."

DAYS 1–10 — EXECUTING (with Daily Scrum):
Daily Scrum (15 minutes, same time each day):
Dev 1: "Yesterday: completed UPI ID validation logic. Today: starting bank lookup API integration. Blockers: NPCI sandbox credentials not received yet."
Scrum Master Rahul: "I'll escalate the NPCI credentials to Priya — she has the bank contact." [IMPEDIMENT REMOVED by end of day]

Day 6 — Mid-sprint concern:
The transaction limit feature is proving more complex than estimated (security review needed). Rahul alerts Priya. Priya decides: remove transaction limit from this sprint, add push notifications instead (smaller scope). Sprint Backlog adjusted — Sprint GOAL unchanged.

DAY 10 — SPRINT REVIEW (≤2 hours for 2-week sprint):
Attendees: Scrum Team + Product Manager, 2 business stakeholders
Demo: Priya demos account linking and payment flow on a staging device
Stakeholder feedback: "The PIN screen is confusing — can we add a visual keyboard?" → Added to Product Backlog. "Great work on the speed."
Product Backlog updated with new items

DAY 10 — SPRINT RETROSPECTIVE (≤1.5 hours for 2-week sprint):
Attendees: Scrum Team ONLY (no stakeholders)
Went well: "Good collaboration on the NPCI issue; resolved in 4 hours"
Improvement: "We underestimated regulatory complexity of the transaction limit feature — next time, regulatory stories need a 'complexity spike' before sizing"
Action: "Before next sprint, do a 2-point spike on daily limit regulatory requirement" — assigned to Dev 2 by Day 2 next sprint

Increment produced: Account linking + UPI payment + last 10 transactions + push notifications. All meet DoD. Potentially shippable.

Kanban Board — Reading and Acting on It

Team: API Platform support team (continuous flow — no sprints)
WIP limits: Backlog(∞) → Analysis(3) → Development(4) → Code Review(2) → Testing(3) → Done

Current board state:
Backlog: 12 items
Analysis: 3 items ✅ (at limit)
Development: 4 items ✅ (at limit)
Code Review: 6 items ❌ (limit is 2 — 3× over limit)
Testing: 1 item ✅
Done: 8 items today

What the board is telling you:
Code Review is a BOTTLENECK. Work is piling up waiting for review. Development keeps pushing code, but it cannot move forward. The whole system is backing up.

What to do — Kanban response:
1. STOP pulling new items into Development (even if WIP allows it)
2. SWARM on Code Review — developers who are waiting for review help with reviews
3. Ask: WHY is code review slow? (Reviewer unavailable? Review criteria unclear? Items too large?)
4. In this case: the two assigned reviewers are both blocked by a production incident. Solution: PM escalates to free up one reviewer; another developer gets temporarily upskilled for review

Lead Time analysis: Items are now averaging 8 days from start to done. The Code Review bottleneck is adding 3 days of wait time.
Process Cycle Efficiency = Actual work time / Total lead time = 5 days / 8 days = 62.5% — good, but could be better.

Key Kanban insight: The bottleneck is ALWAYS the right place to focus. More work upstream of a bottleneck = more waste. Always work from the RIGHT side of the board first.

User Story — Writing and Breaking Down

Epic: International Money Transfer Feature

User Story (well-written):
"As an NRI customer, I want to transfer money to an Indian bank account from my UK account so that I can support my family without visiting a bank branch."

Acceptance Criteria (using Given-When-Then):
• Given I am logged in and my UK account is verified,
  When I initiate a transfer of £100 to an IFSC-linked account,
  Then the transfer is processed within 2 business days and I receive a confirmation email with reference number.
• Given exchange rates fluctuate,
  When I initiate a transfer,
  Then the system shows me the live exchange rate and locks it for 30 minutes.
• Given a transfer fails due to incorrect IFSC,
  When the system detects the error,
  Then I receive an error message within 5 seconds with the specific reason and a correction guide.

INVEST criteria check:
✅ Independent — can be tested and delivered without other stories
✅ Negotiable — the 30-minute rate lock is a business decision, can be changed
✅ Valuable — directly solves an NRI customer need
✅ Estimable — team can size it (likely 13 story points)
✅ Small — fits within one sprint
✅ Testable — acceptance criteria are clear and testable

Definition of Done for this story:
Code reviewed ✅ | Unit tests written (>85% coverage) ✅ | Integration test with UK banking API passed ✅ | Compliance team signed off ✅ | Deployed to staging ✅ | PO accepted in sprint review ✅
