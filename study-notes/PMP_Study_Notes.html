How to study with these notes

The PMP exam (Exam Content Outline) rewards the next professional action, not quoting a book. Use this file as a teaching layer: understand the idea, then close the laptop and say it in one sentence.

  1. For every messy stem, run A.R.T. — Assess the real problem, Review the agreements and artifacts, Take the smallest honest action.
  2. Name the approach first: predictive, adaptive, or hybrid. The right tool changes with the approach.
  3. Name the domain second: is this mainly governance, scope, time, money, people, resources, or uncertainty?
  4. Drill math until SPI, CPI, EAC, float, and communication channels are automatic.
  5. Full-length mocks and sleep beat rereading. These notes are dense on purpose — cycle them, do not binge once.
What “refer, don’t copy” means here: section titles follow the Eighth Edition map (foundations → value → principles → life cycles → seven domains → tailoring → tools → PMO/AI/procurement). Paragraphs are original teaching, examples, and exam traps. They are not a condensation of PMI’s sentences.

Exam snapshot (public ECO)

180 items, 230 minutes, two optional 10-minute breaks, mixed item types (choice, multi-select, matching, hotspot, limited fill-in). A few items are unscored pretest. PMI does not publish a raw passing score. Aim for Target or Above Target in every domain.

DomainShareWhat it feels like on the exam
People~42%Lead, coach, conflict, stakeholders, virtual teams, psychological safety
Process~50%Plan, deliver, measure, change, risk, quality, close — any approach
Business Environment~8%Benefits, compliance, external change, org change

Across those domains, expect roughly half the scored work to feel plan-driven and half agile or hybrid. A hybrid stem often has a hard constraint (law, contract date) plus uncertain product work.

1. Foundations — what you are managing

Purpose of a standard (in exam language)

A standard describes professional consensus: language and expectations so a sponsor in one country and a team in another can still talk. It is not a law, not a contract, and not a recipe that you follow blindly. Tailoring is expected. If an answer treats “the book said so” as a reason to ignore safety, ethics, or a unique constraint, it is wrong.

Key ideas you must not mix up

  • Project — temporary, unique result, then it ends. If work has no end, it is probably operations.
  • Project management — applying knowledge, skills, tools, and leadership so the result creates intended value, not merely activity.
  • Product — the thing users keep using after the project. Product thinking cares about ongoing value; project thinking cares about a bounded effort to change something.
  • Program — related projects coordinated so benefits appear that you would miss if you ran them separately.
  • Portfolio — mix of projects, programs, and operations judged against strategy. “Should we even fund this?” is often a portfolio question.

Characteristics of a project

Temporary does not mean short. A five-year construction job is still a project if it has a start, an intended end, and a unique outcome. Unique does not mean nobody has ever built a house; it means this house, this constraint set, this stakeholder map. Progressive elaboration is normal: you know more later than you knew at charter time. That is why baselines get more precise and why change control exists.

Governance: organization vs project

Organizational governance is how the enterprise decides, funds, audits, and holds people accountable. Project governance should plug into that: who can approve money, who can accept scope, who sits on a change board, when a gate can kill the work. If the project invents a shadow approval path that the organization never recognized, you will fail audit and exam items about escalation.

Exam: when a VP “just wants a small change,” you still use the organization’s change path. Rank is not a substitute for governance.

Operations vs projects

Operations keep delivering the same capability (payroll, support, manufacturing line). Projects change capability (new payroll system, new plant). Handover is where they meet. If you never plan transition, training, and support, the “successful” project becomes an operational disaster — and that is a Business Environment miss as much as a Process miss.

2. A system for value delivery

Creating value

Outputs are not benefits. A deployed app is an output. Faster claims processing or new revenue is a benefit. Value can be financial or not (safety, reputation, learning, compliance). The exam punishes finishing original scope after the benefit case has died. Sunk cost is not a professional reason to continue.

Success is judged by the people who asked for the change and by the organization that funded it. “We hit the Gantt chart” with a product nobody uses is failure. Adaptive teams often measure success with customer outcomes and working increments; predictive teams still need benefit owners, not only variance reports.

Organizational structures and PM power

If people report to a functional manager, your authority is borrowed. In a weak matrix you negotiate for every hour. In a strong matrix or project-oriented shop you can actually direct work. The exam loves “who assigns the engineer?” — answer with the structure, not with wishful servant-leadership poetry.

PMO types: supportive (templates, coaching), controlling (must use the framework), directive (PMO supplies the project managers). Customer-centric PMOs exist to help teams deliver value, not to collect unused dashboards.

Product management overlap

When the project is one slice of a long-lived product, a product owner or product manager may own the backlog and value. You still own integration, constraints, and team health. Do not steal backlog order from the product owner in a Scrum stem. Do not ignore the CCB in a baselined predictive stem.

Functions you will see in stems

Oversight and coordination

Integrate workstreams, resolve cross-team collisions, keep decisions visible.

Feedback

Reviews, demos, surveys, support tickets — then change the plan with facts.

Facilitate and support

Servant leadership: rooms, decisions, impediments, safety.

Perform work

The team builds. Grabbing the keyboard as a first move is rarely right.

Apply expertise

Bring specialists; do not fake deep technical answers you do not have.

Organizational direction

Strategy, portfolio, compliance — escalate when the project cannot decide.

Provide resources

People, money, tools, access. Negotiate; do not silently over-commit.

Roles

  • Sponsor / customer / product owner — money, need, or ordered backlog. Titles vary; the accountability does not vanish.
  • Project management team — integration, communication, control, coaching.
  • Project team — delivery. In adaptive work they decide how inside a goal.
  • End users and other stakeholders — keep identifying them. New names appear mid-project; that is a trigger to update engagement, not to shrug.

3. Principles as exam habits

Principles are not extra multiple-choice trivia. They are how you break ties between two “reasonable” answers. The Eighth Edition uses six actionable principles (a tighter set than the previous twelve). Study them as behaviors.

Principle (study label)What good looks likeWhat the exam punishes
Holistic viewYou see side effects on ops, risk, people, and later phasesLocal optimization: crash one task and wreck quality
Focus on valueYou drop or reshape work that does not serve the benefit caseProtecting sunk cost or gold plating
Embed qualityDone means meets requirements; prevention over last-minute inspectionShip known defects to “make the date”
Accountable leadershipIntegrity, trust, shared leadership, psychological safetyBlame, hidden status, heroic overtime as a plan
SustainabilityEnvironmental, social, economic effects in design and benefitsTreating ESG as a closeout poster
Empowered cultureDecisions at the lowest competent level, clear constraintsPM makes every technical choice; team is decoration

Mindset dimensions that show up in good options: proactive, ownership, value-driven. Connect principles to domains: value without finance tracking is a slogan; quality without governance is a wish; empowerment without risk conversations is chaos.

Tie-breaker: if one answer analyzes with the team and updates an artifact, and the other emails the sponsor immediately, pick the first unless safety, law, or true lack of authority forces escalation.

4. Life cycles, approaches, cadence, focus areas

Phases and gates

A phase is a bundle of work with a purpose and exit criteria (design complete, factory accepted, increment releasable). A gate is a decision: continue, change, or stop. Gates are where the business case can still kill the work. Skipping a gate because “we are late” is how organizations fund zombies.

Development approaches

RequirementsStable enough to baselineDiscovered while buildingSome locked, some learned

DeliveryOften later, in larger packagesFrequent usable slicesCadence matches the constraint

Choose using three lenses: deliverable (how well do we know it?), project (risk, size, novelty), organization (governance, contracts, culture). Fashion is not a lens.

Delivery cadence

Single delivery, multiple deliveries, or a regular heartbeat. Cadence answers “when does someone get value?” not “did we write a plan?” You can plan a lot and still deliver often. You can also deliver often with almost no useful plan — the exam does not admire that.

Five focus areas (process groups renamed for practice)

These are overlapping kinds of work, not a waterfall of five boxes. You initiate a phase, plan inside execution, and control from day one.

  • Initiating — Why this? Who is the sponsor? Who else matters? What authority does the PM have? Charter-level clarity.
  • Planning — How will we create and protect value? Approaches, estimates, engagement, risk, quality, resources. Planning is iterative.
  • Executing — Coordinate people and sellers, grow the team, communicate, implement responses, produce increments or packages.
  • Monitoring and controlling — Compare to what you agreed, forecast, recommend correction, run change control, validate acceptance.
  • Closing — Formal acceptance, contracts, knowledge, transition, release people, archive. Order matters: do not disband before acceptance is real.

Verb leaks: plan/estimate/define (planning); manage/conduct/implement/acquire (executing); monitor/control/validate/measure (M&C); finalize/close/transition (closing).

Governance performance domain

Governance is the glue: decision rights, integration, much of quality, value oversight, and change. If nobody can say who may spend, who may accept, and who may stop the work, you do not have a project — you have a hobby with a budget.

Value creation and models

Governance should make value visible: benefits owner, measures, and a way to stop work that no longer pays. Models range from lightweight (adaptive product with a product owner) to heavy (steering committee, stage gates, regulated CCB). Match the model to risk and regulation, not to the PM’s personal taste.

Metrics and mechanisms

Useful mechanisms: charters, plans, baselines, issue/risk logs, change log, quality metrics, benefit measures, gates. Useless mechanisms: status theater with no decisions. Predictive environments add stronger baseline discipline and documented change. Adaptive environments still have working agreements, Definition of Done, and a product owner — that is governance.

Work you actually do (original process language)

  • Help the sponsor authorize the effort and the PM’s authority (charter-level).
  • Integrate subsidiary plans into one management approach and freeze baselines when the approach requires it.
  • Direct and coordinate work; manage knowledge while people are still here.
  • Monitor performance; route change through the agreed path; close with acceptance and handover.
All baseline changes need impact analysis (scope, time, cost, quality, risk, benefits) and the agreed approver. “Tiny” and “from a VP” do not skip this. In Scrum, backlog order is the product owner’s; contractual or baselined commitments still need the formal path.

Tailoring and interactions

Light governance for a five-person internal tool; heavy for nuclear, medical, or fixed-price public work. Governance talks to every other domain: a scope add is a finance and schedule event; a quality escape is a stakeholder and risk event.

Check results

Decisions are logged. Baselines match reality or changes are approved. Benefits still have an owner. People know how to escalate. Closeout artifacts exist.

A senior stakeholder says skip the CCB for a small add. First move?
Explain the path, capture a change request, assess impact. Do not implement first.

Scope performance domain

Scope is the agreed set of product and project work — and the exclusions. Unspoken extras are still extras. Traceability means every requirement has an origin, a design home, a test, and a reason it matters.

Key concepts

  • Collect requirements from people who use, fund, operate, or constrain the result (workshops, interviews, observation, prototypes, documents).
  • Define product vs project scope and what is out.
  • Decompose (WBS or backlog hierarchy: theme → epic → story → task).
  • Validate = customer/sponsor acceptance of deliverables.
  • Control = compare work to the agreement; stop silent growth.

Predictive scope baseline ≈ scope statement + WBS + WBS dictionary. Adaptive acceptance is continuous against Definition of Done and product-owner feedback. Gold plating (“we added a feature to be nice”) is still out of scope.

Tailoring

Stable, contractual scope → more decomposition up front. Uncertain product → thin slices and frequent validation. Regulated products → requirements that are legally non-negotiable belong in Done, not in a parking lot.

Interactions

Scope drives schedule and finance. New stakeholders invent new scope — that is a stakeholder-domain trigger. Quality criteria live in requirements and Done. Procurement SOWs are scope in seller language.

Check results

Accepted deliverables match the agreement. Traceability is not a dead spreadsheet. Unrequested work is not hiding in the increment.

Client is unhappy and wants an accelerated schedule with no extra budget. First?
Talk through implications and options (scope, time, cost, quality). Do not crash first when money is forbidden, and do not cut corners.

Schedule performance domain

A schedule is a model of when work can realistically finish given dependencies, calendars, and risk — not a wish posted on a wall.

Building the model

Define activities or backlog slices → sequence (FS most common; also SS, FF, SF; leads overlap, lags wait) → estimate duration → develop the network → control against it.

Critical path = longest path; delay here delays the finish; total float is often zero. Total float = LS − ES (or LF − EF). Free float = slip that does not delay the immediate successor. Watch near-critical paths when you compress.

Compression

Crash = add resources on the critical path (usually cost). Fast-track = overlap sequential work (usually risk). Neither is “do first” if you have not analyzed or negotiated tradeoffs with the person asking for speed.

Adaptive teams often forecast with throughput or velocity. Do not weaponize velocity across teams.

Forecasts are updated. Buffer is not a secret. The critical path is known this week, not last quarter.

Finance performance domain

Finance is funding, reserves, actuals, forecasts, and benefits — not only “did we overspend the spreadsheet.”

Reserves

Contingency = known-unknowns, usually inside the baseline, PM-managed. Management reserve = unknown-unknowns, management-owned, not yours until released. Do not hide gold plating in either bucket.

Earned value (must be fluent)

PV what the plan said you should have earned by now. EV budgeted value of work actually finished. AC what you spent. EV is the anchor.

SV = EV − PV   CV = EV − AC   SPI = EV/PV   CPI = EV/AC
EAC typical = BAC/CPI   remain at plan = AC+(BAC−EV)   broken estimate = AC + fresh ETC
ETC = EAC−AC   VAC = BAC−EAC   TCPI to BAC = (BAC−EV)/(BAC−AC)

Negative SV/CV or index below 1 is unfavorable. TCPI above 1 means remaining work must be more efficient than the past — if that is fantasy, reset the forecast instead of lying.

Early ROM ranges are wide; later definitive ranges should be tighter. Match estimate quality to how much you actually know.

Worked examples

  • EV 80k, PV 100k, AC 90k → SV −20k (behind), CV −10k (over).
  • BAC 200, CPI 0.8 → typical EAC = 250.
  • SPI 1.1 and CPI 0.8 → ahead of earned-value schedule, over cost.

Check results

Forecasts match reality. Benefits still have numbers and owners. Funding gates are not surprises.

Stakeholders performance domain

Anyone who can affect or is affected by the work is in play. Identification is continuous. New names mid-project are a planning trigger, not a trivia fact.

Engagement

Map power and interest (or salience). Move people along unaware → resistant → neutral → supportive → leading. Communications live here: push (email, reports), pull (repo, intranet), interactive (meetings, calls). Sensitive conflict: live conversation. Formal written for contracts and baselines.

Communication channels = n(n − 1) / 2

6 people → 15 channels. 8 people → 28. Adding two people is not “just two more stand-up voices.”

Conflict

Preferred order: collaborate / problem-solve → compromise → smooth (temporary) → force (safety/legal/emergency) → withdraw (almost never first). Talk privately when emotions are high, then bring parties together.

Check results

No surprise “we never heard of this project.” Resistant powerful people have a plan, not a hope. Meeting load matches need.

Resources performance domain

People, equipment, materials, facilities, and a large share of procurement. RACI: exactly one Accountable per activity. Resource calendars tell you when someone is actually available, not when the Gantt wishes they were.

Teams

Tuckman: forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning. Storming is normal — facilitate it. Motivation theories (Maslow, Herzberg, McGregor X/Y, McClelland) show up as label items; situational items still want servant leadership: remove impediments, grow skill, do not publicly shame.

Virtual teams need explicit hours, tools, and decision rights they helped write. Psychological safety is a quality practice: people hide defects when they fear punishment.

Sellers (see also procurement)

Make-or-buy, SOW, fair competition, contract type matches scope clarity. FFP when the work is clear (seller holds cost risk). Cost-reimbursable when it is not (buyer holds more cost risk). T&M needs a cap and attention. Ethics: do not leak one bidder’s price to another.

Check results

The team is not heroically burning out as the “plan.” Sellers are managed to the SOW. Skills gaps have a training or pairing response, not a surprise at demo day.

Risk performance domain

Risk = uncertain future (may, might, could). Issue = already happening. Different logs. When a risk occurs, it becomes an issue.

Flow

Plan how you will handle uncertainty → identify continuously → qualitative (probability/impact, urgency) → quantitative when the money or safety justifies it (EMV, trees, simulation) → choose responses → implement → watch. Every risk needs an owner.

Threats: escalate (outside your authority), avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept (active with a contingency plan, or passive). Opportunities: escalate, exploit, share, enhance, accept. Residual remains after you respond. Secondary is a new risk born from the response itself.

EMV = probability × monetary impact

Example: 20% chance of a $50,000 loss → $10,000 EMV. That is not an issue yet.

Check results

The register is alive. Responses have owners and dates. You are not “accepting” catastrophic safety risk with a shrug.

“We might run out of testers next month.” First artifact?
Risk register, then analyze. Not the issue log.

Tailoring

Tailoring is principled adaptation, not skipping documentation because a poster said “agile.” Why tailor? Context differs: regulation, team skill, novelty, contract, culture. What to tailor: life cycle and approach, which processes you run and how heavy they are, and how you engage people.

A practical four-step loop

  1. Select an initial approach — predictive, adaptive, or hybrid based on uncertainty and constraints.
  2. Tailor for the organization — PMO rules, gates, security, brand, unions.
  3. Tailor for this project — size, novelty, team, geography, risk.
  4. Improve while running — retrospectives, quality escapes, cycle time, stakeholder complaints.

Diagnostics: if WIP is exploding, add limits. If defects escape, tighten Done. If the team is in meetings all day, cut ceremony. If auditors cannot follow decisions, add just enough governance. Each domain can be light or heavy independently — a hybrid often keeps finance and compliance predictive while keeping product scope adaptive.

Inputs, outputs, tools (study method)

Do not memorize every table in the guide. For each chunk of work remember:

  • Trigger — why would I start this?
  • Typical inputs — charter, plan, logs, EEFs, OPAs, agreements, measurements.
  • Typical outputs — updated plan, change request, accepted deliverable, lesson, forecast.
  • Tools that match uncertainty — expert judgment and facilitation everywhere; data analysis when numbers decide; prototyping when users must see; bidding when you buy.
If two tools look right, pick the one that fits the approach in the stem (CCB vs product owner; network diagram vs kanban board).

PMO (appendix map)

A PMO pays for itself by making it easier to start, run, and learn from projects: templates, coaching, portfolio visibility, skilled PMs in a directive model. Customer centricity means the PMO serves project teams and sponsors, not the other way around. Maturity is about reliable value, not more mandatory slides. If the PMO cannot explain how it shortens time-to-value or reduces surprise, it is overhead.

Artificial intelligence (appendix map)

Treat AI as a tool in the environment: drafting, clustering risks, summarizing status, suggesting test cases. Adoption needs data quality, skills, and a use-case that actually helps. Responsible use: privacy, bias, intellectual property, confidentiality, and human accountability for decisions. AI does not approve baseline changes, does not replace stakeholder agreement, and does not get to hide a defect. Prompting can be part of communication skill; it is not a substitute for facilitation.

If a stem offers “let the model decide the vendor” or “generate fake consensus,” reject it. Ethics still win.

Procurement (appendix map)

Make-or-buy: cost, capacity, control, risk, intellectual property, ongoing support. Strategy: what you will buy, how you will select, what contract type. Bid documents: RFI (information), RFQ (price), RFP (solution). Selection criteria must be stated before you open bids, then applied fairly.

Claims: facts, contract, ethics. Legal action is sensitive and last. You represent the organization; you do not freelance a lawsuit in chat. Emerging contracts sometimes mix agile delivery with commercial terms — still: fairness, written agreement, change path.

Agile, Scrum, and ECO domains

The exam is still ~50% adaptive/hybrid even while you study an Eighth Edition map. Agile is an umbrella: iterative and incremental under uncertainty. Manifesto: individuals, working product, customer collaboration, responding to change — over (not instead of) process, heavy documents, contract combat, and a frozen plan.

Kanban: visualize, limit WIP, manage flow. Lead time = request to done. Cycle time = start to done. Throughput = items per time. Continuous flow, no required sprint.

XP flavor: TDD, pairing, CI, refactoring, small releases — engineering practices that protect quality at speed.

Scrum: empiricism = transparency, inspection, adaptation. Values: commitment, courage, focus, openness, respect. 3 roles, 5 events, 3 artifacts. Product owner: ordered backlog and value. Scrum master: coach, impediments, process health. Developers: how to reach Done. Sprint is a timebox aimed at a Done increment. Review inspects the product with stakeholders; retrospective inspects the system of work.

People (~42%)

Lead by purpose, then match style to skill and will. Coach, support, or (rarely) direct for harm. Conflict: collaborate first. Empower within constraints. Clear impediments this week. Ground rules the team helped write. No public humiliation. Emotional intelligence is a delivery skill.

Process (~50%)

Plan enough to start; keep artifacts honest. Predictive: baselines and CCB. Adaptive: backlog and Done. Hybrid: both, on purpose. Knowledge transfer is a process, not a leftover. Urgency without skipping safety or quality.

Business environment (~8%)

Benefits over sunk cost. Compliance in Done. New law → analyze, then change/risk path. Safety → stop unsafe work first. Org change (training, sponsorship, communication) is part of delivery when users must work differently.

Traps, closure order, mini drills

  • Do first is almost never crash, fast-track, cut scope, or fire someone. Analyze and align unless safety/law.
  • Might/could → risk register. Has happened → issue log.
  • Always/never are usually wrong except safety, ethics, and baseline change control.
  • Live conversation beats a spicy email for conflict.
  • Closure: acceptance → lessons/knowledge → transition → release people.
  • Velocity is a forecast aid, not a ranking weapon.
  • Do not skip the CCB or the product owner “just this once.”

Mini drills

  1. EV 80, PV 100, AC 90 — behind and over.
  2. BAC 200, CPI 0.8 — EAC 250.
  3. Team 6→8 — channels 15→28.
  4. Testers might run out next month — risk.
  5. VP wants a tiny baseline add tonight — change request + impact.
  6. Regulation date + unknown UI — lock compliance; iterate UI.
  7. Backlog order — product owner.
  8. Business case dead — benefits conversation; consider cancel.
  9. ES=5, LS=9 — float 4.
  10. PERT O=2, M=5, P=14 — expected 6.
  11. Public blame, silent team — restore safety, then the technical issue.
  12. Defect minutes before demo — transparency; product owner decides with facts.
