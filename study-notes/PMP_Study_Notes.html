1. What a project is (and is not)

A project is temporary and unique: it creates a product, service, or result, then ends. Operations keep the lights on. Mixing them causes bad answers (treating BAU as a project, or never closing a project).

Governance is how the organization decides, funds, and oversees work. Project governance should line up with that, not fight it.

Portfolio aligns work to strategy. Program coordinates related projects for extra benefits. Project delivers a defined result. Strategy mix → portfolio. Shared benefits across projects → program.

2. Value delivery, environment, and roles

Value is why the work exists: benefits, not busy-ness. Success is more than “on time / on budget.” Ask who receives the benefit and when.

EEFs are conditions you inherit (market, culture, regulations, infrastructure). OPAs are what the organization already owns (templates, lessons, procedures). Tailor inside those constraints; they are not optional.

PMO flavors: supportive (templates), controlling (compliance), directive (runs projects).

  • Sponsor / customer / product owner — funds, value, or backlog order (title depends on approach).
  • Project management team — integrate, coordinate, remove blockers.
  • Team — does the work; in agile they self-manage how.
  • End users and other stakeholders — identify more than once.

Stems often ask who should act, not who has the fanciest title. Functions: oversight, feedback, facilitation, doing the work, expertise, organizational direction, providing resources.

3. Six principles (study labels)

Treat these as habits, not slogans to quote.

  1. Holistic view — every decision has side effects on people, risk, cost, and later operations.
  2. Focus on value — drop or reshape work that does not serve the benefit case.
  3. Embed quality — build it in; do not inspect heroics at the end.
  4. Accountable leadership — integrity, trust, psychological safety; leadership can be shared.
  5. Sustainability — environmental, social, and economic effects belong in the design, not a poster at closeout.
  6. Empowered culture — push decisions to the people closest to the work, within agreed constraints.
Good-answer posture: proactive, ownership, value-driven. Bad answers skip analysis, hide bad news, or dump unsolved problems on the sponsor.

4. Life cycles, approaches, cadence, focus areas

Phase = chunk of work with exit criteria. Gate = go / no-go / change. Focus areas are not the same as phases; you can plan and control inside every phase.

  • Predictive — requirements stable enough to baseline; change control is formal.
  • Adaptive — uncertainty is high; learn by delivering slices.
  • Hybrid — lock what must be locked (law, contract date); keep empirical the uncertain middle.

Choose based on deliverable uncertainty, project risk, and organization culture — not fashion. Cadence is when value shows up (one delivery, many, or a heartbeat), not whether you planned.

5. Performance domains (seven)

These absorb older knowledge-area labels. You still do integration, quality, communications, and procurement — the map moved.

Governance

Glue of the project: purpose, decision rights, integration, much of quality, and change control. Charter authorizes; the sponsor issues it (you may help write it). The plan integrates subsidiary plans and baselines.

Change control: after baselines exist, every baseline change needs impact analysis and the agreed path (often a CCB). Rank does not skip process.

Close in order: formal acceptance → knowledge transfer / lessons → transition → release people. Do not disband first.

Scope

Requirements have an owner and a test. Trace them. Predictive baseline = scope statement + WBS + WBS dictionary. Validate = acceptance with the customer. Control = stop silent growth. Gold plating is still extra scope. Adaptive: product owner orders the backlog; Definition of Done is the quality contract.

Schedule

Define → sequence → estimate → develop → control. Critical path is the longest path (usually zero total float). Crash = more resources (cost). Fast-track = overlap (risk). Neither is step one if you have not analyzed or talked about tradeoffs.

Estimating: analogous (fast, coarse), parametric (rate × quantity), three-point / PERT, bottom-up (slow, usually most accurate).

Finance

Broader than cost tracking: funding, reserves, benefits, forecasts. Contingency (known-unknowns) sits with the project inside the baseline. Management reserve (unknown-unknowns) sits with management until released.

EVM anchors: EV earned, PV planned, AC spent.

SV = EV − PV    CV = EV − AC
SPI = EV / PV    CPI = EV / AC
EAC typical = BAC / CPI    remain at plan = AC + (BAC − EV)
ETC = EAC − AC    VAC = BAC − EAC    TCPI to BAC = (BAC − EV) / (BAC − AC)

Index < 1 or negative variance is unfavorable.

Stakeholders

Identify again when new people appear. Map power/interest. Move people toward the engagement you need (unaware → resistant → neutral → supportive → leading). Communications: push / pull / interactive. Sensitive conflict: live conversation. Channels = n(n − 1)/2.

Resources

People, equipment, materials, and much of procurement. RACI: one Accountable per activity. Tuckman storming is normal — facilitate it. Servant leadership: remove impediments this week. FFP when scope is clear; cost-plus when not; T&M with a cap. Do not shop one bidder’s price to another.

Risk

Risk = future and uncertain. Issue = already happening. Different logs. Flow: identify continuously → qualitative (and quantitative when it pays) → respond → implement → watch.

Threats: escalate, avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept. Opportunities: escalate, exploit, share, enhance, accept. Residual remains after a response; secondary is born from the response. EMV = probability × money.

6. Tailoring

Tailoring is deliberate, not “we skipped docs because we are agile.”

  1. Pick a starting approach (predictive / adaptive / hybrid).
  2. Adjust for the organization (governance, PMO, culture).
  3. Adjust for this project (risk, team skill, constraints).
  4. Inspect (retrospectives, issues, quality, stakeholder feedback) and adjust again.

If quality is falling or the team is drowning in ceremony, that is a tailoring signal.

7. Inputs, outputs, tools (how to study them)

Do not memorize every ITTO table. For each domain remember a trigger, a few typical inputs (charter, plan, logs, EEFs/OPAs), a few typical outputs (updated plan, change request, accepted deliverable), and tools that match uncertainty.

Expert judgment and meetings everywhere; data analysis when numbers matter; facilitation when people disagree. If two tools look right, pick the one that fits the approach in the stem.

8. PMO, AI, procurement extras

PMO earns its keep by making it easier to deliver value — not by becoming a paperwork factory.

AI is a tool: data quality, privacy, bias, and human accountability still belong to the project. Useful for drafting or clustering risks — not for secretly changing baselines or faking agreement.

Procurement: claims need facts and ethics. Legal fights are last, not first. Contract type is a risk-allocation choice.

9. Agile / Scrum (still half the exam)

Agile is an umbrella. Manifesto: people, working product, customer collaboration, responding to change — over (not instead of) process, heavy docs, contract fights, and a frozen plan.

Kanban: visualize, limit WIP, manage flow. Cycle time = start to done. Throughput = items per time.

Scrum: transparency, inspection, adaptation. 3 roles, 5 events, 3 artifacts. Product owner orders the backlog. Scrum master coaches and unblocks. Developers own how to get to Done.

People: coach, collaborate first on conflict, ground rules the team helped write, no public humiliation.

Process: keep artifacts honest; hybrid honors both the CCB and the backlog.

Business environment: dead business case → pause/pivot/cancel. New law → analyze then change/risk process. Safety → stop unsafe work first.

11. Exam traps

  • Do first ≠ crash / fast-track / cut scope. Analyze and align people first unless safety or law.
  • Might / could = risk register. Has happened = issue log.
  • Always / never are usually wrong except safety, ethics, and “all baseline changes go through change control.”
  • Live conversation beats a spicy email for conflict.
  • Do not fire, cancel a contract, or skip the CCB as a first move.
  • Closure: acceptance before lessons and before releasing the team.
  • Velocity is a forecast aid, not a weapon to compare teams.

12. Mini drills

  1. EV 80, PV 100, AC 90 — behind and over (SV −20, CV −10).
  2. BAC 200, CPI 0.8 — typical EAC = 250.
  3. Team grows 6 → 8 — channels 15 → 28.
  4. “Might run out of testers next month” — risk, not issue.
  5. VP wants a “tiny” baseline add tonight — change request + impact.
  6. Hybrid: regulation date + unknown UI — plan the legal wrapper; iterate the UI.
  7. Product backlog order — product owner.
  8. Business case dead — talk benefits with the sponsor; consider cancel.
